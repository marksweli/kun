## Abstract

Under high-temperature adverse environments, the premature failure of air plasma spray thermal barrier coatings (APS-TBCs) is a preliminary phenomenon that can significantly limit the application of TBCs in gas turbine engines. The delamination failure of TBCs typically occurs at the interfaces between the topcoat and bond coat due to thermal mismatch stress and thermal gradient, resulting in crack propagation and final spallation failure of the coating. This paper undertakes a study of the delamination of TBCs using multi-physics methodologies. Heat transfer was cyclically implemented into the TBC model, resulting in a thermal gradient, to simulate the in-service operation of the TBC system. A variational-based sintering model for a topcoat of TBCs is incorporated into the simulation. The high-temperature creep model of the topcoat, thermal growth oxide (TGO) and bond coat is included. The stress field across the TBCs was calculated during the thermal cycles, with the location of high-stress concentration selected as the potential crack initiation site. Phase field damage modelling was conducted to study crack propagation, initially located at the high-tensile stress off-peak interface. Results indicate that at the off-peak interface, the crack propagates rapidly in the top right direction during the first few cycles, then stops propagating as the TGO thickens, because the location of the maximum principal stress is moved away from the interface. Since the accumulated stress at the crack tip at the end of cycles, cracks have the potential to propagate with a prolonged thermal cycle service.

**Keywords:** Thermal barrier coating · Sintering · Thermal gradient · Stress distribution · Phase field damage

## 1 Introduction

For the latest aero and industrial gas turbine engines, the demand for better efficiency requires a higher inlet gas temperature by at least \(100-150^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\), reducing the substrate temperature significantly to extend the lifespan of hot section components by minimizing the creep and oxidation damage (Perepezko 2009). However, the current Ni-based single-crystal superalloys have reached their limit in terms of temperature capabilities after decades of development. Therefore, thermal barrier coatings (TBCs) have long been developed to reduce the temperature of the metal substrate and air-cooling systems by ventilating within the internal duct and bleeding the air to generate an air film on the blade surface (Padture et al. 2002; Cao et al. 2004; Han 2004). Essentially, the TBC features a ceramic thermal insulating topcoat (TC) for thermal barrier and an oxidation-resistant metallic bond coat (BC) to bond the ceramic TC to the metallic superalloy substrate. During the thermal cycles, a thermally grown oxide (TGO) layer forms between the TC and BC. To date, the TBCs can be deposited by either air plasma spray (APS) process or electron beam physical vapour deposition (EB-PVD) techniques, the latter being more often used for aero-engine applications because the EB-PVD TBCs microstructure is more tolerant to thermo-mechanical fatigue (Evans et al. 2001).

The BC is a crucial component of the TBC system, with either platinum (Pt) modified nickel aluminate, \(\beta\text{-(Ni, Pt)Al}\), or a multiphase \(\mathrm{MCrAlY}\) (\(\mathrm{M}=\mathrm{Ni}\) and/or Co) alloy as the most commonly used. This BC allows the formation of an alumina \((\mathrm{Al_2O_3})\) TGO layer at the TC/BC interface, protecting both the BC and the substrate from further oxidation during subsequent service exposure. The topcoat, consisting of yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ), is equally important. Initially, in the as-deposited state, the TBC is well bonded to the substrate upon which it is being deposited; however, as the coating system is continuously exposed to operating temperatures, its adherence degrades. To date, the prime-reliant TBCs have not been fully realized (Darolia 2013).

The failure of TBC commonly occurs due to delamination near the TC/TGO interface, where stress accumulates as a result of thermal mismatch. Since the atmospheric plasma spray (APS) TBC with porous structure consists of thousands of horizontal microcracks, the large-scale delamination may initiate with the propagation of these microcracks (Baker and Seiler 2017). It is generally believed that the failure process of TBCs starts in the earlier stage of TBC service; these initial cracks propagate throughout the coating layer thickness and reach the coating/substrate interface. Eventually, the coating and substrate separation would be large enough to cause large-scale buckling in edge delamination (Evans et al. 2001; Zhang et al. 2017, 2019, 2021a, b). Although the failure mechanisms of TBCs have been extensively studied, they remain incompletely understood due to the complex interplay of multiple factors contributing to failure. To this end, further research is needed on the failure of TBC systems.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 briefly introduces the methodology and the materials models used in the simulations. Section 3 evaluates the stress distribution and evolution near the TC/TGO/BC interface by combining creep relaxation, TGO oxidation growth, and TC sintering, where a high stress concentration is identified as a potential crack initiation site. In Sect. 4, the phase-field damage model was combined with the crack propagation simulation near the off-peak of the interface. A comparison of the stress distribution with the existing analysis from the literature is also discussed to validate the calculated results. The findings of this study on the delamination phenomenon of TBCs, represented by crack propagation, using multi-physics methodologies, can contribute to the development of more robust TBCs, thereby enhancing the efficiency and lifespan of gas turbine engines. Conclusions are made in Sect. 5.

## 2 Methodologies and materials models

### 2.1 Sintering model of APS-TBC topcoat

Due to a prolonged high-temperature exposure, the ceramic topcoat YSZ experiences severe sintering. This sintering alters the topcoat microstructure, resulting in an increase in density, which leads to changes in mechanical properties, including enhanced elastic modulus and increased hardness, accompanied by a reduction in toughness. These changes affect topcoat behaviour, thus affecting the durability of the entire TBC system. Therefore, it is necessary to study the sintering mechanisms and their effect on TBC failure behaviour and lifespan (Zhang et al. 2021a, b).

At the early stage, the effect of YSZ sintering was ignored in FEM stress analysis on TC/TGO interface (Ranjbar-Far et al. 2010; Rösler et al. 2001, 2004; Song et al. 2021). Of existing sintering formulae, a constitutive sintering model developed via the variational principle would be a more comprehensive option for FEM simulations. Cipitria et al. (2007, 2009a, b) developed a disk-like periodic unit of YSZ splat with an inter-splat bridge to connect other splats and a brick model to represent the intra-splat microcracks. Based on the variational principle, the balance energy \(\Pi\) between the free energy reduction rate \(\dot{G}\) (also known as sintering force) and energy dissipation \(\Psi\) (or material viscosity) can be established as (Cipitria et al. 2007):

\[
\Pi = \dot{G} + \Psi
\tag{1}
\]

To keep the system stable, the difference between dissipation of energy and consumption of free energy is minimized when a time step is infinitesimal:

\[
\delta \Pi = \delta(\dot{G} + \Psi)
\tag{2}
\]

In Cipitria’s sintering model (Cipitria et al. 2009a, b), Young’s modulus is fixed during the sintering process, which is impractical for the actual scenario and overestimates the life prediction model. Cocks and Fleck (Cocks and Fleck 2010) attempted to add an empirical knock-down factor to estimate the effective Young’s modulus as a fraction of densified YSZ Young’s modulus \(\mathrm{E_0}\) based on the asperity spacing at the penny-shaped cracks that are distributed in the densified YSZ. Recently, Lv et al. (Lv et al. 2019) developed a more practical method to simulate the elastic modulus of the YSZ TC by considering elastic, visco-plastic, and sintering stress. To reflect all these factors, Lv et al. (2016, 2019) used a constitutive model to determine the stress \((\sigma)\)–strain \((\epsilon)\) relationship for a thermal elasto-viscoplastic model represented by three terms:

\[
d\epsilon = \{d\epsilon^{E_i}\} + \{d\epsilon^{V_p}\} + \{d\epsilon^{\mathrm{sint}}\}
\tag{3}
\]

where \(\mathrm{E_i}\) is elastic, \(\mathrm{V_p}\) is viscoplastic and \(\mathrm{sint}\) is sintering. As sintering proceeds, the microcracks will be healed and densified as a dense solid. According to Lv et al.’s simulation (Lv et al. 2016, 2019), the elastic modulus grows from \(110\) to \(170\ \mathrm{GPa}\), where \(170\ \mathrm{GPa}\) is the elastic modulus of bulk YSZ (Cocks et al. 2014).

In practice, the elastic modulus of TC is significantly lower, ranging from \(60\) to \(80\ \mathrm{GPa}\) due to the \(15-25\%\) porosity existing within TC generated during the fabrication process. In addition, the elastic modulus may also depend on defect characteristics such as cracks and their distributions. When the actual measurement of elastic modulus is taken, these defects can affect the measured values; therefore, we refer to the actual measured value as the macroscopic modulus. Using the Lv et al. constitutive model (Lv et al. 2016, 2019), our sintering-induced macroscopic modulus increases as sintering time and temperature decrease, Table 1. It is referred to as a time-temperature-dependent model that best describes the evolution of material properties. Additionally, the sintering rate varies at different temperatures, and elastic modulus contours are generated for each sintering temperature. The time-dependent elastic modulus of YSZ was determined by in situ measurement, as it needs to be conducted at elevated temperatures (Qi and Yang 2011). The results are listed in Table 1, which outlines the model used in the present simulation.

### 2.2 TGO growth model

An empirical model, fitted to a phenomenological oxide growth process, is introduced by Busso et al. (2001a, b). The total thickness \(\mathrm{h_{TGO}}\) of the oxide layer with temperature \(T\) and time \(t\) is expressed as:

\[
h_{\mathrm{TGO}} = A_0 t^m \exp\left(-\frac{Q_0}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T}-\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)\right)
\tag{4}
\]

where \(A_0\) is a proportional constant, \(m\) is the growth exponent, \(Q_0\) is the activation energy, \(T_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is the reference temperature at \(2424\ \mathrm{K}\) and \(T\) is the input temperature at the TGO. The BC alloy is easily oxidized; therefore, an average of \(1\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) BC is oxidized before TC is deposited (Baker et al. 2005; Song et al. 2021). With this model, the TGO spends around \(100\ \mathrm{h}\) growing to \(6\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) and around \(5000\ \mathrm{h}\) growing to \(10\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) at \(1293\ \mathrm{K}\), which matches the expected lifespan of YSZ TBC coatings (Busso et al. 2001a, b). The parameter values of the equation are given in Table 2. The TGO starts growing when the coating reaches the service temperature and pauses when the heating period ends. The TGO growth model is achieved by material switching from BC to TGO during the heating period. The creep model eliminates the stress induced by the material switch at elevated temperatures. Considering the time cost of simulation and the significance of the TGO growth to the stress distribution, the growth rate is accelerated to \(6\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) in the first \(30\ \mathrm{h}\) at \(1343\ \mathrm{K}\).

### 2.3 Creep model of TC, TGO and BC

Except for the superalloy substrate, the elastic-viscoplastic creep behaviour of TC, TGO, and BC was considered in the current simulation of the TBC system. Under the thermal load at a temperature higher than \(873\ \mathrm{K}\), the TC, TGO, and BC can constantly reshape their microstructure and relax the residual stress (Pan et al. 2003). The experiment has demonstrated that the creep behaviour is fitted with the Norton power law, and this model has been widely applied in multiple TBC system failure studies with FEM analysis (Rösler et al. 2001; Bialas 2008; Kyaw et al. 2013; Jiang et al. 2018; Abdelgawad and Al-Athel 2021; Song et al. 2021). The governing equation for creep behaviour is described as Eq. (5):

\[
\dot{\epsilon}_{cr} = \frac{\partial \epsilon}{\partial t} = A_{\mathrm{nor}}\sigma^n
\tag{5}
\]

where \(\dot{\epsilon}_{cr}\) is the creep strain rate, \(A_{\mathrm{nor}}\) is the creep rate coefficient, \(\sigma\) is the residual stress, and \(n\) is the stress exponent. Since the creep effect only occurs at high temperatures, the creep rate coefficient becomes active when the temperature exceeds \(1123\ \mathrm{K}\). The creep data of TC, BC, and TGO are given in Table 3.

### 2.4 Phase field damage model

The failure behaviour, such as the crack propagation of TBCs, is affected by complex factors. In this paper, the phase-field damage model was applied, which has properties: (a) it is independent of the mesh; (b) the crack path does not need to be predefined. The primary difference is that the phase-field damage model characterizes a sharp crack as a diffusive crack, governed by the phase field variable, which ranges from \(0\) to \(1\). When the phase-field variable equals \(1\), the material is fully damaged; when it equals \(0\), it is intact. At the crack topology, the variable equals \(1\) at the center of the crack surface and gradually moves to \(0\) at the width of the diffusive length scale (Zhou et al. 2018b).

Miehe et al. (Miehe et al. 2010) introduced the phase field diffusive crack propagation method of finite element analysis without modelling discontinuities in the displacement field. This built-in feature in COMSOL Multiphysics enables the coupling of multifield finite element solvers. For a sharp crack surface \(\Gamma\) in the body \(\mathcal{B}\) seen in Fig. 1, a crack phase field variable \(d(x)\in[0,1]\) is introduced to describe the crack damage condition. Parameter \(d\) is a scalar that describes the material’s status. A simple model of a 1-D bar with a crack at \(x=0\) visualizes the phase field variable at the sharp and diffusive cracks. In this case, the phase field variable is approximated as the exponential function:

\[
d(x) = e^{-|x|/l_{\mathrm{int}}}
\tag{6}
\]

where \(l_{\mathrm{int}}\) is the length scale used to regularize the sharp crack topology \(\Gamma\) and it has the Dirichlet-type boundary condition: \(d(0)=1\) and \(d(\pm\infty)=0\). For details, the formulation and explanation regarding the phase field damage model can be seen in (Miehe et al. 2010).

## 3 FEM model of TBC system and stress state

To conduct the phase field damage modelling, the stress distribution and its evolution in TBCs under thermal cycles can be used to initiate the crack seed setup. COMSOL Multiphysics, featuring multi-physics coupling, can combine solid mechanics with heat transfer formulae. With COMSOL Multiphysics, a thermal gradient can be modelled across the thickness of the TBC–superalloy system. Additionally, the power-law creep, sintering, and oxide growth models are well-integrated. The finite element model geometry, boundary conditions, heat transfer model setup, and mesh design are outlined below.

### 3.1 FEM model setup, geometry, and boundary conditions

In the present paper, the interface is described by a sinusoidal curve with reference configuration: height \(a=10\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) and wavelength \(w=24\ \mu\mathrm{m}\). The geometry is close to the one used by (Rösler et al. 2001). The initial thickness of TGO \(h_{\mathrm{TGO}}=1\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) and it increases at each thermal cycle by switching the BC layer to TGO (Zhang et al. 2021a, b). The TGO growth model is described by expression (4) and model parameters in Table 2. The thickness of (a) \(\mathrm{TC}=250\ \mu\mathrm{m}\); (b) \(\mathrm{BC}=130\ \mu\mathrm{m}\); (c) substrate \(=5\ \mathrm{mm}\) are used. Figure 2 illustrates the reference configuration of the FEM geometry, along with the specified boundary conditions.

For the boundary conditions, only half of the periodic unit is simulated to conserve computational power, and the other half utilizes a symmetry boundary. A roller is applied to the bottom of the substrate to avoid stress concentration at the corners. Air pressure is applied to the top surface of TBC. A prescribed displacement is applied to the right surface of the model to simulate the lateral thermal expansion in a periodic unit. The prescribed displacement is determined by the weighted CTE of each layer of material; the expression gives:

\[
\delta = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathrm{CTE}_i \cdot 12[\mu\mathrm{m}] \cdot b_i[\mu\mathrm{m}] / 5380[\mu\mathrm{m}]
\tag{7}
\]

where \(12\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) is the model’s width, \(5380\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) is the total thickness of the system, \(\mathrm{CTE}\) is the coefficient of thermal expansion at each layer, and \(b_i\) is the thickness of each layer. Mesh elements constrain the prescribed displacement; therefore, the thermal gradient can affect the prescribed displacement due to the varying thermal expansion strains in thickness. In our case, the substrate dominates the thermal expansion in the in-plane direction, and thermal stress \(\sigma_{11}\) will be induced by heating and cooling each layer.

### 3.2 Heat transfer model setup

Usually, in the furnace cycling test of TBCs, it is difficult for the thermal gradient to be realized in experimental conditions (Rösler et al. 2001; Kyaw et al. 2013; Shinozaki and Clyne 2013; Bäker and Seiler 2017). However, thermal gradient could play an essential role in transient states, and the CTE mismatch can also cause in-plane stress during heating and cooling periods. Given these factors, it is necessary to discuss the effect of thermal gradient in our FEM modelling.

The thermal gradient is incorporated into our model, leveraging the built-in Multiphysics feature of COMSOL. For aero-engine applications, the blades are protected by the TBC system and the air cooling system. The air temperature first drops due to the cooling air film, and then the TC further decreases the temperature because of its low thermal conductivity. The total temperature drop with cooling air and TC is greater than \(200\ \mathrm{K}\). The temperature will slightly decrease at the metallic BC and the substrate. The heat will be removed through internal ducts, allowing cooling air to flow constantly.

With the given information, the heat transfer model can be built into our geometry model in Fig. 2, which demonstrates that the heat will be transferred from the exterior hot gas with an inward heat transfer coefficient \(h_{\mathrm{in}}\), then conducting through the layers of material, then dissipating with cooling air, with a cooling heat transfer coefficient \(h_{\mathrm{cool}}\). The side walls of the periodic unit are set as insulated. The heat conduction within the model is governed by Fourier’s law:

\[
\mathbf{q} = -k\nabla T
\tag{8}
\]

where \(\mathbf{q}\) is the local heat flux density in \(\mathrm{W/m^2}\), \(k\) is the thermal conductivity in \(\mathrm{W/mK}\), and \(\nabla T\) is the temperature gradient in \(\mathrm{K/m}\). Newton’s law of cooling dominates the heat transfer from air to solid at the boundary:

\[
q = -h\Delta T
\tag{9}
\]

where \(h\) is the heat transfer coefficient in \(\mathrm{W/m^2K}\) and \(\Delta T = T_1 - T_2\) is the temperature difference between the surrounding fluid and the solid surface area. For the scenario of heat transfer by external forced convection, the inward heat transfer coefficient is governed by Eq. (10) (COMSOL 2020a):

\[
h_{\mathrm{in}} =
\begin{cases}
2\dfrac{k}{L}\dfrac{0.3387\mathrm{Pr}^{1/3}\mathrm{Re}_L^{1/2}}{1+\left(\dfrac{0.0468}{P}\right)^{2/3}}, & \mathrm{Re}\leq 5\cdot 10^{5} \\[8pt]
2\dfrac{k}{L}\mathrm{Pr}\left(0.037\mathrm{Re}_L^{4/5}-871\right), & \mathrm{Re}>5\cdot 10^{5}
\end{cases}
\tag{10}
\]

where \(\mathrm{Pr}=\mu C_p/k\) is Prandtl’s number and \(\mathrm{Re}_L=\rho U L/\mu\) is Reynolds’ number, \(k\) is the air conductivity, \(L\) is the blade length, \(U\) is the airspeed, and \(P\) is the external pressure. \(C_p\) is the air heat capacity and \(\mu\) is the viscosity. The governing equation is typically based on the airspeed \((U)\), blade length \((L)\) and external pressure \((P)\). For the \(h_{\mathrm{cool}}\) at the cooling duct, considering the complex internal structure (like ribs, fins), it is hard to express it as an equation. The experimental result read at \(\mathrm{Re}=55000\), \(h_{\mathrm{cool}}=420\ \mathrm{W/m^2K}\) (Wang et al. 1996). For cooling air bleed from the compression stage, the temperature reads \(T_{\mathrm{cool}}=650^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) (Boyce 2012).

Thermal properties of each layer are listed in Table 4, and the environmental inputs include the airspeed \(U\), blade length \(L\), inlet pressure \(p\), hot gas temperature \(T_{\mathrm{out}}\), and cooling air temperature \(T_{\mathrm{cool}}\) are listed in Table 5. The thermal cycle applied in the simulation is illustrated in Fig. 3, where the heat transfer input is represented by the red line and the isothermal input by the blue line. Each cycle lasts \(3600\ \mathrm{s}\), or \(1\ \mathrm{h}\), with \(3100\ \mathrm{s}\) of heating and \(500\ \mathrm{s}\) of cooling to ensure it is fully cooled down. The thermal cycles will repeat 30 times until the oxide grows to a certain thickness. The thermal gradient applied to the FEM model is shown in Fig. 2. At the surface of TC, the temperature drops from \(1323\) to \(1313\ \mathrm{K}\) with the thin layer of stagnation airflow. Then, the temperature rapidly drops to \(1270\ \mathrm{K}\) at the TC/TGO interface due to the thermal barrier performance. The curve slope decreases along the TGO, BC, and substrate layers. At the bottom of the substrate, the cooling air will carry out the heat energy through heat convection.

### 3.4 Mesh design

The mesh design for the TBC system is shown in Fig. 5. In 2D geometry, the quad element is selected for improved precision, and the total number of generalized plane strain elements is \(5172\). The mesh for the TGO layers that will switch from BC to TGO during the thermal cycles is refined with a mapping technique to ensure the resulting quality. The complexity of the stress requires higher-order elements to ensure the quality of the result. Therefore, the quadratic quad element (8-nodes) is selected for the displacement field, and the linear quad element (4-nodes) is selected for the heat transfer field. In COMSOL Multiphysics, the displacement and heat transfer fields are solved separately using the segregated approach. The segregated solver divides the problems into two steps and then solves them individually. These steps are smaller than the fully coupled approach.

### 3.5 Stress field evolution of TBCs

The stress field and its evolution of TBCs are calculated with the aim of initiating crack seeds in TBC layers. Essentially, locations with a high concentration of stress field are selected as the initiation points for crack seeds, followed by implementing the phase field damage calculation to study the crack propagation during the TGO growth under thermal cycling with selected roughness aspect ratios and topcoat sintering.

The stress field during the 30 thermal cycles was examined, focusing on the radial stress \((\sigma_{22})\), the in-plane stress \((\sigma_{11})\), and the first principal stress \((\sigma_1)\) represented by Cauchy stress tensors, which are components that potentially lead to horizontal and vertical crack propagation. The definition of the first principal stress is:

\[
\sigma_1 = \frac{\sigma_{11}+\sigma_{22}}{2} + \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_{11}-\sigma_{22}}{2}\right)^2 + \tau_{12}^2}
\]

(Irgens 2008). Figure 6 shows the positions of the stress states to be evaluated. All the sampling points are \(0.1\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) above the interface.

Figure 7a and b plot the first principal stress \(\sigma_1\) radial stress \((\sigma_{22})\) along the off-peak and off-valley positions during the 30-h thermal cycles and at the reference aspect ratio (AR) of amplitude \(10\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) / wavelength \(24\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) (10/24). For better readability, at every cycle, the stress decreases to a low level when the TBC system is heated and held; once the system cools down, the thermal mismatch induces residual stress to a high level, and the process repeats in subsequent cycles.

The stress pattern of the first principal stress \(\sigma_1\) at the off-peak and off-valley locations shows a different trend. At the off-peak position, the first principal stress \(\sigma_1\) is always in a tensile state. The stress level increases in the first two cycles to about \(220\ \mathrm{MPa}\), then gradually decreases to \(140\ \mathrm{MPa}\), and then holds steady in the last 10 cycles. For the stress during heating, the stress level decreases from \(50\) to \(0\ \mathrm{MPa}\), primarily due to creep relaxation. At the off-valley position, the first principal stress \(\sigma_1\) is in the compressive state in the first 10 cycles, then turns to a tensile state and progressively grows in the subsequent 20 cycles to about \(80\ \mathrm{MPa}\). Compared to the stress at the off-peak, the off-valley stress level is always lower, which means that the initial cracks near the peak pose a more significant threat to propagate. However, it is necessary to determine the stress components, such as radial and in-plane stress, to identify how cracks propagate.

For the radial stress \(\sigma_{22}\), its maximum value at the off-peak position occurs in the first cycle, at about \(140\ \mathrm{MPa}\), then rapidly decreases in the first 15 cycles. It is mainly affected by the rapid growth of TGO, then secondly relaxed by the creep deformation. As mentioned above, the horizontal cracks in APS-TBCs are observed and related to the radial stress \(\sigma_{22}\). The large \(\sigma_{22}\) in the first few cycles is a driving force for horizontal crack propagation. At the off-valley, the radial stress shows a negative trend compared to the off-peak position. For example, the \(\sigma_{22}\) becomes tensile after cycle 14 and reaches a value of \(60\ \mathrm{MPa}\) when the TGO thickness is \(5.4\ \mu\mathrm{m}\). Then, it becomes the major location for crack nucleation and propagation for horizontal cracks. Our current simulation on stress distribution and evolution, shown in Fig. 7 at the off-peak and off-valley positions, is consistent with the literature.

## 4 Crack propagation using phase field damage model

This section examines the crack propagation of APS-TBCs using the phase field damage model in response to various parameters, including interfacial roughness, TGO growth rate, thermal gradient, and sintering. The multi-physics approach couples the conventional heat transfer with the phase field damage model. The phase field damage model can simulate the crack propagation independent of the mesh by applying a phase field variable from \(0\) to \(1\) to determine how much the material is damaged. If the material is damaged in an iteration, the damaged elements in the crack are simulated as degraded material in the next iteration. This method benefits from modelling the crack without discontinuity and is a trending method for damage estimation.

### 4.1 Phase field model setup, geometry, and boundary conditions

In this phase-field damage simulation, the same geometry as in Sect. 3 is used. Additionally, the cracks are created following the cracks in Sect. 3 regarding the stress state evaluation and the SEM images (Essa et al. 2021) of the APS-TBC testing specimen, showing that horizontal cracks are distributed around the TC/TGO interface. With this model, a crack with an initial length of \(0.5\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) is implemented at the off-peak site in Fig. 9 to examine the crack propagation behaviour under different conditions. The crack tip near the interface side is rounded to form concave corners, thereby avoiding stress concentration at the sharp corner.

### 4.2 Phase field model parameters and mesh design

The material properties used in this section are the same as in Sect. 3. The mesh design for this section is an extension of the geometry in Fig. 5. Since the initial crack is implemented, a refinement is necessary near the crack tip to ensure the convergence of the phase field damage model. The width of the initial crack is set at \(0.06\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) with a square-shaped crack tip. The square crack tip design follows the examples of the notched concrete beam with a three-point bending test and 2D notched square plates subject to shear load (Zhou et al. 2018a; Wu and Huang 2020). The internal length scale \(l_{\mathrm{int}}\) is set to \(0.06\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) as well, to ensure the diffusion zone is large enough to avoid discontinuity issues. The maximum mesh element size at the potential crack path is set as \(h_{\mathrm{elem}}=l_{\mathrm{int}}/2=0.03\ \mu\mathrm{m}\). Given so, in COMSOL, the minimum element will be automatically adjusted to a value \(<h_{\mathrm{elem}}\) to ensure the precision of topology, as suggested by Zhou et al. (2018a). ### 4.3 Parametric study

A series of parametric studies is necessary to better validate the phase field crack simulation results regarding crack propagation. #### 4.3.1 Crack propagation near the off-peak of TC

In the preceding sections, the FEM analysis demonstrates that tensile stress is distributed near the off-peak region. This section examined the crack growth behaviour of a horizontal crack at the off-peak position. The results for the crack propagation are presented in two parts: the phase field crack topology and the damaged first principal stress distribution during the crack propagation. The damaged first principal stress is defined as the first principal stress multiplied by the phase field variable \(d\) to describe the stress state at the damaged nodes precisely.

Figure 10 demonstrates the phase field crack propagation process at selected times and cycles for case A in Table 7. At cycle 1 and \(3700\ \mathrm{s}\) the crack grows towards the top right direction for about \(0.58\ \mu\mathrm{m}\). In the stress plots, Fig. 11 shows that the stress at the crack tip moves as the crack propagates. At cycle 2 and \(7300\ \mathrm{s}\) the crack length continually grows to \(1.48\ \mu\mathrm{m}\). The stress plots indicate that the first principal stress at the peak is reduced, which is attributed to the growth of TGO. The growth of TGO shifts the location of the maximum first principal stress down along the interface. Additionally, the crack propagation at cycle 2 relaxed the stress center. At cycle 3, the crack grows to \(1.97\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) and then it stops growing at cycles 7. In the stress plots, the stress magnitude reduces rapidly at the peak and the crack tip. Additionally, the location of maximum stress shifts to the mid-level of the roughness, as illustrated in Fig. 11. Based on the results, the crack will stop propagating due to the stress magnitude at the crack tip gradually reducing with thermal cycles.

#### 4.3.2 Phase-field damage and stress evolution versus roughness ratios

Figures 12 and 13 show the phase field evaluation and the first principal stress \(\sigma_1\) contour during the crack propagation process for case B with \(\mathrm{AR}=15/24\). The crack propagates towards the top right direction, like in reference case A, but it doesn’t go up as much as in reference case A. At the end of cycle 1, the crack length is \(0.6\ \mu\mathrm{m}\). At cycle 2, the crack length increases to \(1.46\) and \(1.85\ \mu\mathrm{m}\); then it stops propagating until cycle 30. Spotting the stress distribution plot, the stress concentration zone moves with the crack tip. After cycle 3, the principal stress center gradually shifts to the mid-level of the roughness until cycle 30, at which point the stress at the crack tip is relaxed.

The phase field crack propagation and damaged first principal stress results for case C with AR (5/24) are shown in Figs. 14 and 15. The crack did not propagate at cycle 1. Then, it starts to propagate in cycle 2 towards the top right direction at a short distance and larger angle relative to the initial crack orientation. The crack length at \(0.20\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) then stops propagating in the remaining cycles. This can be explained by the first principal stress plots. At cycle 1, the stress center is at its peak position, which prevents the crack from propagating. In contrast, at cycle 2, the location of the maximum first principal stress moves to the crack position and propagates a short distance. Afterward, due to the continued movement of the crack center, the stress level at the crack tip is reduced, and the crack cannot be further propagated.

#### 4.3.3 Phase-field damage evolution versus initial crack positions

In case D, the crack was initially located in the mid-level of the rumpling, and after running, the results will be compared to those in the reference case A. The crack phase field and damaged first principal stress for case D are shown in Fig. 16. The legend ranges from \(0\) to \(0.2\) in the crack phase field, indicating that the initial crack has not fully propagated (fully damaged at phase field \(=1\)) but is partially damaged at the crack tip. As the stress plots display, the stress center for this case gradually shifts from the peak position to the valley, but the maximum stress level decreases as it moves toward the valley. Therefore, it is unable to propagate the crack. This result agrees with Fig. 7, which shows that the maximum first principal stress decreases from \(220\ \mathrm{MPa}\) in cycle 1 to \(140\ \mathrm{MPa}\) in cycle 10, rendering the crack unable to propagate. Even though the stress level increases in the latter stage with sintering, it cannot propagate the crack to the mid-level.

Normally, the crack propagation path derived from the phase field damage model in TBCs cannot be directly and quantitatively validated by SEM images. However, its stress field can be compared with the stress contour plots that exclude the crack. The maximum first principal stress \(\sigma_1\) in Fig. 11, initially located near the peak position, is decreased and gradually moves away from the peak position as the cycle proceeds. The stress magnitude is decreased more than in the model without a crack involved because the crack propagation consumes energy. In Fig. 13, with full thermal cycles, the maximum \(\sigma_1\) moves further to the mid-level from cycle 5 to cycle 30. Also, \(\sigma_1\) in the crack tip is reduced in the later cycles, and then the crack stops propagating. The higher the AR ratio, the higher the stress is at the early stage, as shown in Figs. 13 and 15. It is noteworthy that the present results are similar to those of Jiang et al. (2018), where the XFEM method was conducted. In their simulation, a horizontal crack was implemented at the off-peak position, and the crack propagation criteria were the principal stress. The results showed that the cracks only propagated in the 1st cycle during the dwell and cool-down period and then stopped propagating. The final length is about \(20\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) in a sinusoidal interface with \(80\ \mu\mathrm{m}\). The length is longer than our current model (which is \(\sim 2\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) in \(24\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) wavelength), but it is consistent because the critical stress for the Jiang et al. model is lower, and the aspect ratio is much smaller.

This section simulates local crack propagation during thermal cycles using the phase field damage method with selected associated parameters and conditions, such as different roughness AR values and crack locations. In reference case A, the crack at the off-peak position grows towards the top right direction in the first 2 or 3 cycles. Then, the crack stops propagating because the location of maximum principal stress is moved away. With different aspect ratios AR, the final crack length and the propagation direction vary. But in all cases, the crack grows fastest at cycles 2 and 3 because the maximum stress at TC is very close to the crack. The crack cannot continue moving to the right end in the model due to the decrease in the stress magnitude upon TGO growth. Therefore, it can be assumed that the local cracks initiated at the off-peak position do not damage the entire coating layer. When relocating the crack to the mid-level of the roughness, the crack did not propagate during the 30-h thermal cycles, which is reasonable considering that the maximum principal stress decreases as the TGO thickness increases. However, the stress accumulated at the crack tip at cycle 30 can be observed, which has the potential to propagate when the criterion changes. The purpose of this section is not only to simulate crack propagation but also to validate the feasibility of using the phase field damage model to simulate crack propagation at TC. In further studies, scenarios such as different geometries, multiple crack interactions, and varying crack orientations can utilize this method to estimate the coating’s failure or lifespan.

However, it is still necessary to indicate the limitations of the current methodology. As the constant parameters, such as the critical strength \(\sigma_c\) was used in phase field damage modelling on crack propagation, it is difficult to directly compare the simulated crack path with experimental observations because, first of all, the experimental \(\sigma_c\) evolves with composition, coating layer morphologies and phase transformation. Additionally, our current simulation of crack propagation is limited to 30 cycles due to the available computational capability, which makes it difficult to compare with the testing cycles that typically run until 1000 thermal cycles.

## 5 Conclusions

This paper presents the COMSOL multi-physics simulation of TBCs, focusing on stress distribution and crack propagation, while considering TC sintering, BC oxidation, creep relaxation, and heat transfer through the thickness. The stress distribution and its time evolution near the TC/TGO interface were used as a potential crack nucleation site to study crack propagation using phase field damage methodology.

Results show that the first principal stress \(\sigma_1\) concentrates at the peak position in early cycles and then moves to the mid-level along TGO in late cycles. The stress magnitude decreases as the TGO thickens. However, after halfway through the thermal cycle, the stress magnitude increases again due to TC sintering. The results indicate that TGO thickness is the primary factor contributing to stress concentration. With a higher aspect ratio of the interface, the stress center is less affected by the TGO thickness; however, the stress magnitude at the early cycles increases due to additional thermal mismatch. The phase field damage model results demonstrate that the crack near the off-peak position propagates at the early thermal cycles and stops propagating as the stress center moves to the valley. The stress distribution matches those in stress analysis and demonstrates the feasibility of using the phase field damage model to estimate crack growth during the thermal cycles. The method can be further implemented in other geometries with different crack locations, orientations, or numbers of cracks to estimate the crack propagation and life estimation.
