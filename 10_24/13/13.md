## ABSTRACT

The foreign particle erosion that the particles ingested into the gas turbine engine inlet or produced by the carbon deposits in the combustor could impact the surface of ceramic layer in thermal barrier coatings (TBCs) may cause the performance loss of TBCs and engine explosion in severe cases. Thus, it is important to study the foreign particle erosion-induced failure mechanism in thermal barrier coatings. To this end, we propose a coupling plastic damage model of dual-horizon peridynamics and FEM for foreign particle erosion-induced failure of TBCs. Dual-horizon peridynamics is used in the portions of areas that may exist significant plastic deformation and damage, meanwhile FEM is used in the remaining areas to minimize computing costs. The coupling plastic damage model is firstly validated by the ductile damage of the 2D asymmetrically notched specimen and 3D cylinder with the initial penny-shaped fracture. And then, the influences of the foreign particle shape, impact velocity and erosion angle on the erosion failure of TBCs are comprehensively investigated by the coupling plastic damage model. The numerical results are in quantitative and qualitative agreement with the existing experiment or the preceding numerical solution. Our numerical investigation confirms the need for developing the coupling plastic damage model in revealing the foreign particle erosion-induced failure process of TBCs.

## 1. Introduction

Because of their abilities to shield the alloy substrate from extremely high temperatures, thermal barrier coatings (TBCs) are frequently employed in the gas turbines and aviation engine turbine blades [1–3]. Typical TBCs consist of three layers: TC (Y₂O₃-stabilized ZrO₂ ceramic top coat) layer, BC (bond coat) layer and TGO (thermally grown oxide) layer. Because of their complicated structure and extreme service environment, TBCs are prone to be damaged for a variety of reasons, such as the high-temperature interface oxidation [4,5], the foreign particle erosion (FPE) [6,7], the corrosion by CMAS (calcium-magnesium-aluminum-silicate) [8]. As the primary cause of the coating peeling failure, the interface oxidation process and its failure mechanism have been extensively studied by means of experiment and numerical simulation [4]. As the second major cause of the failure of TBCs, the FPE has been paid more and more attention [7,9–11].

The particles ingested into the gas turbine engine inlet from volcanic ash clouds, storm clouds carrying sand and dust etc. or produced by the carbon deposits in the combustor could damage the gas turbine engine component and cause performance loss [10]. The impacting of these particles on TBCs might cause cracking and fragment removal of the coating surface, which may lead to failure to provide thermal protection to the substrate material. Therefore, the FPE resistance of TBCs should be well investigated. Wellman et al. [12] proposed theoretical model to discuss the influence of the foreign particle size on the contact radius under Hertzian contact conditions. Li et al. [13] established the theoretical relation between the foreign particle erosion resistance of ceramic coating and lamellar structure parameters. Meanwhile, they revealed that the foreign particle erosion resistance was controlled by coating fracture toughness. Fleck et al. [14] provided the analytical solutions to describe the transient stress state within the coating for each deformation mechanism. The above-mentioned theoretical models have done the important contributions to understand the foreign particle erosion of TBCs. Apart from these theoretical models, some numerical models have been recently proposed. Crowell et al. [15] quantitatively compared the experimental impact crater profiles with numerical simulations of equivalent impact scenarios. And then they comprehensively studied and revealed the influence of TBC microstructural parameters and particle yield parameters. Sadowski et al. [16] conducted the numerical and experimental investigations of the foreign particle impact into the coating surface and provided the impacting results with different energies of particles. By means of the scanning electron microscope, the computational model was established by Yang et al. [6] to study the foreign particle erosion behavior of TBCs with the actual morphology. These findings suggest that, in contrast to the earlier theoretical or numerical results with simplified morphology, the actual morphology of TBCs would be more vulnerable to erosion failure. Golewski et al. [17] experimentally investigated the foreign particle erosion caused by large particles with the diameter of 4 mm. They discussed the influence of the coating aging on the crack propagation of TBCs. More useful and important phenomena such as delamination or brittle cracking have been revealed. The damage mechanism of TBCs exposed to a single foreign particle impact was discussed by Zhu et al. [18]. As the foreign particle impact velocity, foreign particle size and impact angle increase, the damage of TBCs is more likely to occur. Yu et al. [9] explored on how the interface morphology affected the foreign particle erosion failure of TBCs. They pointed out that the influence of the interface shapes on the maximum erosion depth of TBCs would be more evident if the kinetic energies of the foreign particles were higher. Song et al. [19] investigated how the foreign particle erosion affected the stress distribution and damage evolution of TBCs. The particle erosion may speed up the TGO layer's development rate even if it has minimal effect on the remaining thermal mismatch stresses. It means that the foreign particle erosion reduces TBCs' ability to insulate against heat. Ning et al. [7] thoroughly studied the influence of the foreign particle shape on the maximum foreign particle erosion depth, the particle rebound velocity and residual stress state in TBCs. Note that the above-mentioned numerical methods are all based on FEM to investigate the corrosion depth of TBCs without considering the surface damage of TC layer and the mass loss caused by the FPE. For the actual situation, the FPE rate that characterizes the erosion resistance of TBCs should measure the coated sample mass loss (or called the mass loss of TC layer) in the particle erosion experiment [10]. Therefore, the damage and mass loss of TC layer should be considered to accurately study the erosion failure mechanism and erosion resistance. To this end, the plastic damage model for the foreign particle erosion-induced failure in thermal barrier coatings should be established.

In recent years, the researchers and engineers have become more and more interested in peridynamics (PD). Unlike the partial differential equations employed in the context of FEM, the peridynamic theory describes the deformation process using the integral equations. This characteristic distinguishes peridynamics from other continuum mechanics in the form of describing the damage initiation and evolution by the peridynamic bond breakage [20]. As a result, it offers several benefits for treating crack propagation problems without requiring complex treatments. These peridynamic properties have allowed the peridynamic theory to be effectively applied to the intricate dynamic and static crack propagation problems [21–24], the thermal-induced damage process [25–28], the corrosion-induced damage process [29–31], the size effects and cross-scale damage process [32,33], the nonlinear material constitutive models [34–37] and the geometrically nonlinear models [38–41]. As with the meshfree method proposed by Rabczuk et al. [42–44], the peridynamic models are suitable for addressing the contact and impact fracture problems [45–54]. Within the framework of peridynamics, the peridynamic differential operator (PDDO) [55–57], nonlocal operator method (NOM) [58–62] and dual-horizon peridynamic correspondence principle (DH-PDCP) [5,28,33,63] are proposed to address the multi-physics and damage problems. To characterize the plastic deformation and fracture, Lee et al. [64] presented the bond-based peridynamic plastic model to deal with the contact and impact damage problems. Nevertheless, bond-based peridynamic model (BPD) suffers from the restriction of Poisson's ratio that cannot distinguish the distortional and volumetric deformations. To solve this restriction, Madenci et al. [65] proposed the plastic model in the context of ordinary state-based peridynamics (OSPD). It was on the basis of the isotropic linear-hardening criteria to describe the plastic deformation. Later, Liu et al. [34] established a nonlinear-hardening plastic model in the context of OSPD to address the plastic deformation and damage process. Since there is no stress and strain concepts in the framework of OSPD, the classical equivalent stress and plastic strain cannot be calculated directly. As a special kind of non-ordinary state-based peridynamics (NOSPD), the peridynamic correspondence model (PDCM) can provide the stress-strain relationship. Therefore, it can incorporate several classical plastic constitutive material models. However, the zero-energy mode problem and high computing cost of PDCM undoubtedly restrict its potential use in dealing with real-world engineering crack problems [63,66,67]. To solve these problems, Liu et al. [67] introduced a coupling model of peridynamics and FEM for the plastic deformation and damage analysis. In which, the material strength damage criterion is simply used. Recently, Bie et al. [63] presented the nonlocal peridynamics-based correspondence model on the basis of DH-PDCP that is free of zero-energy mode. Additionally, they offered a reasonable standard for the peridynamic bond's rupture through incorporating the damage evolution principles of phase-field theory [53,68–70]. The damage criterion for the peridynamic bond will be extended to determine the nonlinear plastic deformation and damage of the peridynamic bond in this paper.

In this paper, we propose a coupling plastic damage model of dual-horizon peridynamics and FEM for foreign particle erosion-induced failure in TBCs. The nonlinear plastic deformation and softening is described by dual-horizon peridynamic correspondence plastic model, meanwhile the damage evolution is described by the nonlocal dual-horizon bond-based peridynamic phase-field model. The numerical algorithm of BFGS (Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno, or called Quasi-Newton algorithm) is extended to address the peridynamics-based coupled equations of damage and deformation in the integral form. To reduce the computational burden, dual-horizon peridynamics and FEM are geometrically coupled by the dual-based coupling approach presented by Bie et al. [71–74]. The coupling plastic damage model takes advantages of the meshfree and nonlocal properties of dual-horizon peridynamics and the computational efficiency of FEM. Dual-horizon peridynamics is used in the portions of areas that may exist significant plastic deformation and damage, meanwhile FEM is used in the remaining areas to minimize computing costs. The coupling plastic damage model is validated by the examples of ductile damage of the 2D asymmetrically notched specimen and 3D cylinder with the initial penny-shaped damage. The erosion-induced failure mechanism of TBCs is comprehensively investigated by the proposed coupling plastic damage model. Especially, the influences of the foreign particle shape, impact velocity and erosion angle on the erosion failure of TBCs are studied in this paper.

## 2. The coupling plastic model of dual-horizon peridynamics and FEM

### 2.1. The review of dual-horizon peridynamic correspondence plastic model

To take advantage of dual-horizon peridynamics to address the plastic fracture problems and avoid zero-energy mode of traditional peridynamic correspondence model. The DHPD-CP proposed by Bie et al. [63] is extended to establish the dual-horizon peridynamic correspondence plastic model (DH-PDCPM). We assume an initial configuration $\Omega_0\subset\mathbb{R}^n\,(n=1,2,3)$ in Fig. 1, the material point in the initial configuration is determined by its coordinates $\mathbf{x}$. It is possible to think of the peridynamics-based deformation gradient $\bar{\mathbf{F}}$ as the transferring function. It can map the reference vector state $\underline{\mathbf{X}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle$ in the initial configuration into the deformation vector state $\underline{\mathbf{Y}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle$. The foundation of the peridynamic theory, which differs from classical continuum mechanics, is the idea that each peridynamic material point $\mathbf{x}$ interacts directly with other material points in its immediate vicinity (that can be defined as the peridynamic horizon $\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{x}}$). The dynamic form of the integral equilibrium equation for DH-PDCPM may be expressed in the context of dual-horizon peridynamics [75,76] as

$$
\rho\ddot{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x},t)=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{x}}}\underline{\mathbf{T}}[\mathbf{x},t]\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\mathrm{d}V_{\mathbf{x}'}-\int_{\mathcal{H}'_{\mathbf{x}}}\underline{\mathbf{T}}[\mathbf{x},t]\langle-\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\mathrm{d}V_{\mathbf{x}'}+\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{x},t) \qquad (1)
$$

where $\rho$ denotes mass density; $\ddot{\mathbf{u}}$ denotes acceleration; $\mathbf{b}$ denotes body force. $\underline{\mathbf{T}}[\mathbf{x},t]\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle$ can be viewed as the direct force vector state on $\mathbf{x}$ exerted by the peridynamic bond $\boldsymbol{\xi}$, meanwhile $\underline{\mathbf{T}}[\mathbf{x},t]\langle-\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle$ can be seen as the reaction force vector state on $\mathbf{x}$ exerted by the peridynamic dual bond $-\boldsymbol{\xi}$ in the context of dual-horizon peridynamics [75,76]. The peridynamic horizon $\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{x}}$ and dual-horizon $\mathcal{H}'_{\mathbf{x}}$ [75,76] related to the peridynamic material point $\mathbf{x}$ are defined as

$$
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{x}}=\{\mathbf{x}'\in\Omega_0,|\mathbf{x}'-\mathbf{x}|\leq\delta_x\};\ \mathcal{H}'_{\mathbf{x}}=\{\mathbf{x}'|\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{x}'}\} \qquad (2)
$$

where $\delta_x$ denotes the horizon length associated with the peridynamic material point $\mathbf{x}$.

The peridynamic force vector state between two peridynamic material points can be attained by computing Fréchet derivative of nonlocal strain energy with respect to the deformation state on the basis of DH-PDCP [5,28,63]. The comprehensive derivation can be found in the reference [63]. The expression of the peridynamic force vector state is given by

$$
\underline{\mathbf{T}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=\underline{\omega}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\cdot\underline{\mathbf{X}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\otimes\underline{\mathbf{C}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\cdot\mathbf{K}^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}+1/2\,\underline{\omega}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\cdot\underline{\mathbf{C}}^m\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\cdot\underline{\mathbf{Z}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle \qquad (3)
$$

$$
\underline{\mathbf{C}}^m\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=k^m\cdot\underline{\mathbf{C}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle;\ \underline{\mathbf{C}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=\boldsymbol{\xi}\otimes\boldsymbol{\xi}/|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^3;\ \underline{\mathbf{Z}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=\underline{\mathbf{Y}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle-\bar{\mathbf{F}}\cdot\underline{\mathbf{X}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle \qquad (4)
$$

$$
k^m=\begin{cases}48E/(5\pi H\delta_x^3)\quad\text{for plane strain}\\ \dfrac{12E}{\pi\delta_x^3}\quad\text{for 3D}\end{cases} \qquad (5)
$$

where $E$ and $H$ denote Young's modulus and thickness, respectively. $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ denotes Cauchy stress that can be written in the form of vector $\boldsymbol{\sigma}=[\sigma_{11};\sigma_{22};\sigma_{33};\sigma_{12};\sigma_{23};\sigma_{13}]$ for the three-dimensional problems.

The expression of the alternative shape matrix $\mathbf{K}$ [63] is given by

$$
\mathbf{K}=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{x}}}\underline{\omega}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\cdot\underline{\mathbf{C}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\otimes\underline{\mathbf{V}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\mathrm{d}V_{\mathbf{x}'};\ \underline{\mathbf{C}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=\underline{\mathbf{V}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle/|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^3 \qquad (6)
$$

For the two-dimensional problems, the state $\underline{\mathbf{V}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle$ is written as

$$
\underline{\mathbf{V}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=[\xi_1\xi_1;\xi_2\xi_2;\xi_1\xi_2]_{3\times1} \qquad (7)
$$

For the three-dimensional problems, the state $\underline{\mathbf{V}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle$ is written as

$$
\underline{\mathbf{V}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=[\xi_1\xi_1;\xi_2\xi_2;\xi_3\xi_3;\xi_1\xi_2;\xi_2\xi_3;\xi_1\xi_3]_{6\times1} \qquad (8)
$$

The Cauchy stress vector $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ in Eq. (3) is calculated as

$$
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\mathbf{D}^e\cdot\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^e=\mathbf{D}^e\cdot(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}-\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^p) \qquad (9)
$$

where $\mathbf{D}^e$ denotes elastic material matrix. $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ denotes the total strain separated into the elastic strain $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^e$ and plastic strain $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^p$.

The exact expression of the peridynamics-based double state $\underline{\mathbf{K}}^m\langle\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rangle$ for the displacement fields is obtained by

$$
\underline{\mathbf{K}}^m\langle\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rangle=\frac{\partial\underline{\mathbf{T}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle}{\partial\underline{\mathbf{Y}}\langle\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rangle}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{B}^T\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\cdot\mathbf{D}^p\cdot\mathbf{B}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle-1/2\,k^m\cdot\mathbf{B}^T\langle\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rangle\cdot\mathbf{K}\cdot\mathbf{B}\langle\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rangle\\ +1/2\,k^m\cdot\underline{\omega}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\cdot\underline{\mathbf{C}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\cdot\delta(\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\zeta})\end{cases} \qquad (10)
$$

$$
\mathbf{B}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=\underline{\omega}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\cdot\mathbf{K}^{-1}\cdot\underline{\mathbf{C}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\otimes\underline{\mathbf{X}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle \qquad (11)
$$

where $\mathbf{D}^p$ denotes the plastic material matrix. $\delta(\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\zeta})$ is defined as

$$
\delta(\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\zeta})=\begin{cases}1/V_{\mathbf{x}'},&\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{\zeta}\\ 0,&\boldsymbol{\xi}\neq\boldsymbol{\zeta}\end{cases} \qquad (12)
$$

where $V_{\mathbf{x}'}$ denotes volume related to the peridynamic point $\mathbf{x}'$.

The material constitutive relation can be linked to the nonlocal peridynamic model by updating the plastic material matrix $\mathbf{D}^p$ in Eq. (10) and the Cauchy stress vector $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ in Eq. (3) with the conventional plasticity theory and the radial return method. We assume a time increment from $t_n$ to $t_{n+1}$, firstly, the elastic trial stress vector $\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{tr}$ can be calculated by

$$
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{tr}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_n+\mathbf{D}^e:\Delta\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \qquad (13)
$$

where $\boldsymbol{\sigma}_n$ denotes the stress vector for the time increment $t_n$. $\Delta\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ denotes the total strain increment that can be obtained by

$$
\Delta\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{x}}}\underline{\mathbf{B}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\cdot\Delta\underline{\mathbf{U}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\mathrm{d}V_{\mathbf{x}'};\ \Delta\underline{\mathbf{U}}=\Delta\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}')-\Delta\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}) \qquad (14)
$$

where $\Delta\underline{\mathbf{U}}$ is the increment of the displacement state associated with the bond $\boldsymbol{\xi}$. $\Delta\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}')$ and $\Delta\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})$ denote the increments of displacement related to the peridynamic material point $\mathbf{x}'$ and $\mathbf{x}$ respectively.

As with the conventional plasticity theory [77], the incremental equivalent plastic strain $\Delta\varepsilon^p$ can be calculated by solving for the following equation using the Newton method

$$
\sigma_{eq}^{tr}-3\mu\Delta\varepsilon^p-\sigma_f(\varepsilon^p)=0 \qquad (15)
$$

where $\sigma_{eq}^{tr}$ denotes the equivalent stress; $\mu$ is shear modulus. $\sigma_f$ denotes the flow stress that is the function of equivalent plastic strain $\varepsilon^p$.

Once the incremental equivalent plastic strain $\Delta\varepsilon^p$ is obtained, the equivalent plastic strain $\varepsilon_{n+1}^p$ at time $t_{n+1}$ can be updated by

$$
\varepsilon_{n+1}^p=\Delta\varepsilon^p+\varepsilon_n^p \qquad (16)
$$

where $\varepsilon_n^p$ denotes the equivalent plastic strain at time $t_n$.

Correspondingly, the stress $\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n+1}$, the plastic strain $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n+1}^p$ and the elastic strain $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n+1}^e$ at time $t_{n+1}$ can be updated by

$$
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n+1}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{dev}^{tr}}{\sigma_{eq}^{tr}}\sigma_f+\boldsymbol{\delta}\sigma_h^{tr} \qquad (17)
$$

$$
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n+1}^p=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_n^p+\frac{3}{2}\Delta\varepsilon^p\frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{dev}^{tr}}{\sigma_{eq}^{tr}} \qquad (18)
$$

$$
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n+1}^e=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}-\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n+1}^p \qquad (19)
$$

where $\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{dev}^{tr}$ and $\sigma_h^{tr}$ denote the stress deviator and hydrostatic stress, respectively. $\sigma_{eq}^{tr}$ is the equivalent stress. $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ is the Kronecker delta. $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_n^p$ denotes the plastic strain at time $t_n$.

Lastly, the plastic material matrix $\mathbf{D}^p$ (or the consistent material Jacobian) can be updated by $\mathbf{D}^p=\partial\Delta\boldsymbol{\sigma}/\partial\Delta\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta\boldsymbol{\sigma}=&\left(K-\frac{2}{3}\mu\frac{\sigma_f}{\sigma_{eq}^{tr}}\right)\mathrm{tr}(\Delta\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})\cdot\boldsymbol{\delta}\\
&+\left(2\mu\frac{\sigma_f}{\sigma_{eq}^{tr}}+\left(\frac{3\mu\,\partial\sigma_f/\partial\varepsilon^p}{3\mu+\partial\sigma_f/\partial\varepsilon^p}-3\mu\frac{\sigma_f}{\sigma_{eq}^{tr}}\right)\frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{dev}^{tr}}{\sigma_{eq}^{tr}}\right):\Delta\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
\end{aligned} \qquad (20)
$$

### 2.2. The coupling plastic model of dual-horizon peridynamics and FEM

Despite having a unique ability for predicting failures, PD is more computationally expensive than FEM. To benefit from their unique characteristics, dual-horizon peridynamics (DHPD) and FEM should be combined [73,74,78]. The effective dual-based coupling approach (DB-CA) [71–74] for the elastic damage problems is extended to couple dual-horizon peridynamics and FEM for the plastic damage problems in this paper. The coupling of dual-horizon peridynamics and FEM can be directly realized by DB-CA without introducing blending function, mapping parameters or overlap zone. The similar idea has been used to couple PDDO and FEM for brittle fracture [78], BPD and FEM for quasi-brittle failure [79], correspondence-based peridynamics and FEM for brittle fracture [66], element-based peridynamic model and FEM for the plastic damage problems [67], by the peridynamic dual bond.

For the integrity of the paper, the dual-based coupling approach for the plastic damage problems are briefly reviewed in the following part. To learn more about DB-CA, readers can refer to the references [71–74]. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the whole computational domain $\Omega_0$ is divided into a FEM subregion $\Omega_1$ and DHPD subregion $\Omega_2$, such that $\Omega_0=\Omega_1\cup\Omega_2$, $\Omega_1\cap\Omega_2=\varnothing$. The subregion $\Omega_1$ is discretized with the finite mesh, meanwhile the subregion $\Omega_2$ is discretized with the peridynamic material point (also called local node). The DB-CA is realized in the form of the dual bonds in Fig. 2. In other word, the coupling can be simply viewed as that the peridynamic points apply the external forces to the finite nodes in the FEM subregion by the dual bonds. There are three different kinds of nodes in the coupling model in Fig. 2 (type "a", type "b" and type "c"). To couple dual-horizon peridynamics and FEM, the unified equilibrium equation [71–74] in the initial configuration can be written as:

$$
m_k\ddot{\mathbf{u}}_k=\mathbf{f}_k^{ext}-\mathbf{f}_k^{local-int}+\mathbf{f}_k^{nonlocal-int} \qquad (21)
$$

where $m_k$ denotes mass associated with node $k$, $\ddot{\mathbf{u}}_k$ denotes acceleration, $\mathbf{f}_k^{ext}$ denotes the external force. $\mathbf{f}_k^{local-int}$ can be seen as the local finite element-based internal force vector obtained by FEM, $\mathbf{f}_k^{nonlocal-int}$ can be viewed as the nonlocal internal force vector obtained as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{f}_k^{nonlocal-int}=&\int_{V_k}\int_{\mathcal{H}_k}\underline{\mathbf{T}}[\mathbf{x}_k,t]\langle\mathbf{x}_j-\mathbf{x}_k\rangle\mathrm{d}V_j\mathrm{d}V_k\\
&-\int_{V_k}\int_{\mathcal{H}'_k}\underline{\mathbf{T}}[\mathbf{x}_k,t]\langle\mathbf{x}_k-\mathbf{x}_j\rangle\mathrm{d}V_j\mathrm{d}V_k
\end{aligned} \qquad (22)
$$

where $\mathbf{x}_j$ and $\mathbf{x}_k$ denote the coordinates of node $j$ and $k$. $V_j$ and $V_k$ denote the volumes of node $j$ and $k$.

(1) For the node $\mathbf{x}_a\in\Omega_1$ colored blue (called type "a" node in Fig. 2), it belongs to local node. It is only subjected to the finite element-based internal forces. The equilibrium equation of the type "a" node is expressed as

$$
m_a\ddot{\mathbf{u}}_a=\mathbf{f}_a^{ext}-\mathbf{f}_a^{local-int} \qquad (23)
$$

(2) For the node $\mathbf{x}_b\in\Omega_1$ colored green (called type "b" node in Fig. 2), it belongs to local node. It is subjected to both the finite element-based internal force and the peridynamics-based internal force exerted by the peridynamic dual-bonds within the dual-horizon of the peridynamic material point $\mathbf{x}_b$. The equilibrium equation of the type "b" node is

$$
m_b\ddot{\mathbf{u}}_b=\mathbf{f}_b^{ext}-\mathbf{f}_b^{local-int}+\mathbf{f}_b^{nonlocal-int} \qquad (24)
$$

Since the horizon length of the local node $\mathbf{x}_b\in\Omega_1$ can be viewed as zero [71,72], the nonlocal internal force vector in this case is totally contributed by the dual bonds

$$
\mathbf{f}_b^{nonlocal-int}=-\int_{V_b}\int_{\mathcal{H}'_b}\underline{\mathbf{T}}[\mathbf{x}_b,t]\langle\mathbf{x}_b-\mathbf{x}_j\rangle\mathrm{d}V_j\mathrm{d}V_b;\quad(\mathbf{x}_j\in\mathcal{H}'_b) \qquad (25)
$$

(3) For the node $\mathbf{x}_c\in\Omega_2$ colored purple (called type "c" node in Fig. 2), it belongs to non-local node. It is subjected to only the peridynamics-based internal forces. The equilibrium equation of the type "c" node is expressed as

$$
m_c\ddot{\mathbf{u}}_c=\mathbf{f}_c^{ext}+\mathbf{f}_c^{nonlocal-int} \qquad (26)
$$

### 2.3. The fracture criterion of the coupling plastic model

The peridynamic model offers a fracture criterion by stopping interactions between two peridynamic points when the peridynamic bond energy or relative stretch reaches its critical value. For BPD and OSPD models, the critical peridynamic bond energy or critical peridynamic bond relative stretch can be computed by connecting it to the material's fracture toughness. However, the team of Prof. Rabczuk [69] reported that the peridynamic bond breakage may lead to a singular shape tensor that ultimately ends the computation for NOSPD. To solve this problem, they incorporated the damage evolution method of nonlocal phase-field model (PFM) into the peridynamic model in the framework of nonlocal operator method (NOM) [69]. In addition to easing smoothness and continuity requirements for the field variables of the computational domains, it offers a logical standard for the peridynamic bond's rupture. Inspired by this idea, Bie et al. [63] proposed the unified nonlocal peridynamics-based phase-field damage theory in the context of DH-PDCP. To comprehensively consider the efficiency and convergence, the damage evolution method of nonlocal phase-field theory is included in the proposed dual-horizon peridynamic correspondence plastic model in the context of dual-horizon bond-based peridynamics (DH-BPD).

To describe the peridynamic bonds' nonlinear deformation and breaking, based on the peridynamics-based phase-field models [68,69], the nonlocal peridynamics-based phase-field $d\in[0,1]$ that represents the damage state of the peridynamic material point is introduced in the context of dual-horizon bond-based peridynamics. Comparing the conventional differential equilibrium equation of phase-field [80,81] and the integral equilibrium equation of DH-BPD [75,76], the integral equilibrium equation associated with the damage field is expressed as

$$
\int_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{x}}}\underline{P}[\mathbf{x},t]\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\mathrm{d}V_{\mathbf{x}'}-\int_{\mathcal{H}'_{\mathbf{x}}}\underline{P}[\mathbf{x},t]\langle-\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\mathrm{d}V_{\mathbf{x}'}+Q_2(\mathbf{x})-Q_1(\mathbf{x})=0 \qquad (27)
$$

$$
Q_2(\mathbf{x})=-\omega(d)\cdot\overline{\mathcal{H}};\ Q_1(\mathbf{x})=G_cd/l_c,\ \omega(d)=(1-d)^2 \qquad (28)
$$

where $l_c$ denotes the length scale and $G_c$ denotes the fracture toughness. $\omega(d)$ denotes the softening function, meanwhile $\omega'(d)$ denotes the gradient of $\omega(d)$. The nonlocal peridynamics-based phase-field flow state $\underline{P}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle$ is obtained using the idea of bond-based peridynamics. The final mathematical expression is

$$
\underline{P}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}k^p\cdot\frac{\underline{d}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle}{|\boldsymbol{\xi}|} \qquad (29)
$$

where $\underline{d}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle$ denotes the non-local phase-field state, $\underline{d}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle=d(\mathbf{x}')-d(\mathbf{x})$. The detailed derivation of peridynamics-based diffusion micro-modulus $k^p$ can be seen in Appendix A.

The so-called history variable $\overline{\mathcal{H}}$ [82] is defined as

$$
\overline{\mathcal{H}}=\max_{[0,t]}(\psi_e^+)+\int\sigma_{eq}\dot{\varepsilon}_{eq}^p\mathrm{d}t \qquad (30)
$$

where $\sigma_{eq}$ denotes the equivalent stress and $\dot{\varepsilon}_{eq}^p$ denotes the equivalent plastic strain rate. The tensile part of the elastic energy $\psi_e^+$ [83] is

$$
\psi_e^+=\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_i^e\right\rangle_+^2+\mu\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_i^e\right\rangle_+^2 \qquad (31)
$$

where $\varepsilon_i^e$ is the $i$th principal elastic strain and $\langle a\rangle_+:=(a+|a|)/2$. $\lambda$ and $\mu$ are two Lamé constants.

The peridynamics-based phase-field double state $\underline{\mathbf{K}}^p\langle\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rangle$ on the basis of bond-based peridynamics is calculated as

$$
\underline{\mathbf{K}}^p\langle\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rangle=\frac{\partial\underline{P}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle}{\partial\underline{d}\langle\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rangle}=1/2\cdot k^p/|\boldsymbol{\xi}|\cdot\delta(\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\zeta}) \qquad (32)
$$

The elastic-plastic deformation and softening are described using the dual-horizon peridynamic correspondence plastic model, meanwhile the damage evolution is described by dual-horizon bond-based phase-field model. Therefore, the plastic deformation and damage are physically coupled in the DHPD subregion $\Omega_2$ by the following integral governing equations

$$
\begin{cases}
\rho\ddot{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x},t)=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{x}}}\omega(d(\mathbf{x})):\underline{\mathbf{T}}[\mathbf{x},t]\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\mathrm{d}V_{\mathbf{x}'}-\int_{\mathcal{H}'_{\mathbf{x}}}\omega(d(\mathbf{x})):\underline{\mathbf{T}}[\mathbf{x},t]\langle-\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\mathrm{d}V_{\mathbf{x}'}+\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{x},t)\\
\int_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{x}}}\underline{P}[\mathbf{x},t]\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\mathrm{d}V_{\mathbf{x}'}-\int_{\mathcal{H}'_{\mathbf{x}}}\underline{P}[\mathbf{x},t]\langle-\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\mathrm{d}V_{\mathbf{x}'}+Q_2(\mathbf{x})-Q_1(\mathbf{x})=0
\end{cases} \qquad (33)
$$

### 2.4. The numerical implementation of the DHPD-FEM plastic model

The initial configuration $\Omega_0$ is discretized with finite mesh for the FEM subregion $\Omega_1$ and the peridynamic material points for the DHPD subregion $\Omega_2$ in Fig. 2. Each peridynamic point $\mathbf{x}_i$ in the DHPD subregion is determined by the peridynamic point number $i$. $V_i$ denotes volume associated with the peridynamic material point $\mathbf{x}_i$ ($\Omega_2=V_1\cup V_2\cdots\cup V_m$ and $\forall j\neq k$, $V_j\cap V_k=\varnothing$). The general peridynamics-based element $E_i$ consists of those peridynamic material points within the horizon of the peridynamic material point $\mathbf{x}_i$ and itself [72,73]. The primary task is to calculate the global stiffness matrix $\mathbf{K}$ and residual $\mathbf{r}^u$ of the DHPD-FEM plastic model. It should be noted that there is only displacement variable in the FEM subregion $\Omega_1$ to describe the continuous elastic-plastic deformation behavior. However, there are displacement and damage variables to describe the elastic-plastic deformation and damage evolution in the DHPD subregion $\Omega_2$, respectively. Therefore, the displacement field $\mathbf{a}^u$ in the whole body $\Omega_0$ is represented as

$$
\mathbf{a}^u=\left\{\underbrace{\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2,\ldots,\mathbf{u}_m}_{DHPD},\underbrace{\mathbf{u}_{m+1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_n}_{FEM}\right\} \qquad (34)
$$

where $n$ denotes the total number of nodes in the body $\Omega_0$, $m$ denotes the total number of nodes in the DHPD subregion $\Omega_2$. Correspondingly, the damage field in the DHPD subregion $\Omega_2$ can be represented as $\mathbf{a}^d=\{d_1,d_2\ldots d_m\}$. The general residual equation of the proposed coupling plastic model in the whole body $\Omega_0$ is expressed by

$$
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{0} \qquad (35)
$$

where $\mathbf{z}=\{\mathbf{a}^u,\mathbf{a}^d\}^T$ denotes the global nodal unknown vector. The residual vector is $\mathbf{g}=\{\mathbf{r}_u^{DHPD},\mathbf{r}_u^{FEM},\mathbf{r}_d^{DHPD}\}^T$. The residual $\mathbf{r}_u^{FEM}$ and stiffness matrix $\mathbf{K}_{uu}^{FEM}$ for the FEM subregion $\Omega_1$ is obtained by FEM. Meanwhile the residuals for the DHPD subregion $\Omega_2$ is calculated as

$$
\begin{cases}
\mathbf{r}_u^{DHPD}=\mathbf{f}^{ext}+\sum_{\mathbf{x}_i\in\Omega_2}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_j\in\mathcal{H}_i}\omega(d)\cdot\underline{\mathbf{T}}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle V_jV_i-\sum_{\mathbf{x}_i\in\Omega_2}\rho\ddot{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x}_i,t)\cdot V_i\\
\mathbf{r}_d^{DHPD}=-\sum_{\mathbf{x}_i\in\Omega_2}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_j\in\mathcal{H}_i}\underline{P}\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle V_jV_i\cdot\eta_{ij}+\sum_{\mathbf{x}_i\in\Omega_2}(Q_2(\mathbf{x}_i)-Q_1(\mathbf{x}_i))\cdot V_i
\end{cases} \qquad (36)
$$

where $\mathbf{f}^{ext}$ is the global external force vector. The symbol $\sum$ denotes the assembly of the peridynamics-based elemental internal force with the same way of FEM. $\sum^{Dual}$ is the dual assembly symbol. Readers can refer to the references [72,73] to know more about the dual assembly algorithm of double state and force state in context of dual-horizon peridynamics. $\eta_{ij}$ denotes the volume correction factor [67] related to material points $\mathbf{x}_i$ and $\mathbf{x}_j$, which is defined as

$$
\eta_{ij}=\begin{cases}1&|\boldsymbol{\xi}|\leq(\delta_{xi}-r_j);\quad r_j=|\boldsymbol{\xi}|/2;\ \boldsymbol{\xi}=|\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_j|\\ \dfrac{\delta_{xi}+r_j-|\boldsymbol{\xi}|}{2r_j}&(\delta_{xi}-r_j)<|\boldsymbol{\xi}|\leq\delta_{xi}\\ 0&|\boldsymbol{\xi}|>\delta_{xi}\end{cases} \qquad (37)
$$

The stiffness matrices for the DHPD subregion $\Omega_2$ is calculated as

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{K}_{uu}^{DHPD}=&\sum_{\mathbf{x}_k\in\Omega_2}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_j\in\mathcal{H}_k}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_i\in\mathcal{H}_j}\omega(d)\cdot\underline{\mathbf{K}}^m\langle\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rangle V_kV_jV_i+\sum_{\mathbf{x}_i\in\Omega_2}\rho\cdot V_i\cdot1/(\beta\Delta t^2)\\
\mathbf{K}_{dd}^{DHPD}=&\sum_{\mathbf{x}_k\in\Omega_2}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_j\in\mathcal{H}_k}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_i\in\mathcal{H}_j}\underline{\mathbf{K}}^p\langle\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rangle V_kV_jV_i\cdot\eta_{ij}\eta_{ik}+\sum_{\mathbf{x}_i\in\Omega_2}(G_c/l_c+2\cdot\overline{\mathcal{H}})\cdot V_i
\end{aligned} \qquad (38)
$$

where $\Delta t$ is the time step. We set $\beta=0.25$ for the reason of the unconditional stability. $\eta_{ik}$ denotes the volume correction factor associated with material points $\mathbf{x}_i$ and $\mathbf{x}_k$.

Since the integral governing equations in Eq. (33) are coupled, the numerical algorithm of Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS) [84] is extended to solve for the DHPD-FEM model. Unlike the conventional Newton method of being updated after a single iteration, the global coupled stiffness matrix $\overline{\mathbf{K}}$ might be updated roughly after several iterations. Additionally, the matrix $\mathbf{K}$ might be substituted by an estimated matrix $\overline{\underline{\mathbf{K}}}$, which must satisfy the relation

$$
\overline{\underline{\mathbf{K}}}(\mathbf{z}^k-\mathbf{z}^{k-1})=\mathbf{g}^k-\mathbf{g}^{k-1}\Leftrightarrow\overline{\underline{\mathbf{K}}}\delta\mathbf{z}=\delta\mathbf{g} \qquad (39)
$$

Moreover, $\overline{\underline{\mathbf{K}}}$ is updated by

$$
\overline{\underline{\mathbf{K}}}=\underline{\mathbf{K}}^{k-1}-\frac{\left(\underline{\mathbf{K}}^{k-1}\delta\mathbf{z}\right)\left(\underline{\mathbf{K}}^{k-1}\delta\mathbf{z}\right)^T}{\delta\mathbf{z}^T\underline{\mathbf{K}}^{k-1}\delta\mathbf{z}}+\frac{\delta\mathbf{g}\delta\mathbf{g}^T}{\delta\mathbf{z}^T\delta\mathbf{g}} \qquad (40)
$$

It is not necessary to compute the matrix inversion if the global stiffness matrix is symmetric. The inversion of $\overline{\underline{\mathbf{K}}}^{-1}$ might be directly updating in the framework of the BFGS method

$$
\overline{\underline{\mathbf{K}}}^{-1}=\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\delta\mathbf{z}\delta\mathbf{g}^T}{\delta\mathbf{z}^T\delta\mathbf{g}}\right)\left(\underline{\mathbf{K}}^{k-1}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\delta\mathbf{z}\delta\mathbf{g}^T}{\delta\mathbf{z}^T\delta\mathbf{g}}\right)^T+\frac{\delta\mathbf{z}\delta\mathbf{z}^T}{\delta\mathbf{z}^T\delta\mathbf{g}} \qquad (41)
$$

The estimated matrix $\overline{\underline{\mathbf{K}}}$ is symmetric if the initial estimated matrix $\overline{\underline{\mathbf{K}}}^{(0)}$ in Eq. (40) is symmetric. Thus, in the framework of BFGS, the uncoupled symmetric stiffness matrix $\overline{\underline{\mathbf{K}}}^{(0)}$ can be set as the initial estimated matrix that has the following form

$$
\overline{\underline{\mathbf{K}}}^{(0)}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{K}_{uu}^{DHPD}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\\\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{K}^{FEM}&\mathbf{0}\\\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{K}_{dd}^{DHPD}\end{bmatrix} \qquad (42)
$$

## 3. Representative numerical examples

In this section, the coupling plastic damage model of DHPD and FEM is firstly validated by the ductile damage of a 2D asymmetrically notched specimen and 3D cylinder with the initial penny-shaped damage. And then, the influences of the foreign particle shape, impact velocity and erosion angle on the erosion failure of TBCs are comprehensively investigated by the DHPD and FEM model in the final part. To provide more details about the convergence and efficiency of the proposed coupling model between dual-horizon peridynamics and FEM, the examples of the dynamic wave propagation and static tensile test are presented in Appendix B.

### 3.1. The ductile damage of a 2D asymmetrically notched specimen

The proposed DHPD-FEM plastic model is verified by the ductile damage of a 2D asymmetrically notched specimen in Fig. 3(a). The monotone increasing displacement is applied at the top surface of the asymmetrically notched specimen, while its bottom surface is fixed. We use the material parameters with Young's modulus of $E=71.48\mathrm{GPa}$, Poisson's ratio of $\nu=0.3$, hardening modulus of $E^H=0.1E$, yield stress of $\sigma_y=345\mathrm{MPa}$ and fracture toughness of $G_c=9.31\mathrm{N/m}$ in the reference [82]. We discretize the DHPD subregion using a fine peridynamic material point with the spacing of $\Delta x=0.06\mathrm{mm}$ in Fig. 3(b). The quadrilateral grid spacing in the FEM subregion is gradually refined from $\Delta x=0.6\mathrm{mm}$ to $\Delta x=0.06\mathrm{mm}$. The peridynamic horizon length and length scale are set as $\delta_x=l_s=3\Delta x$ in the DHPD subregion. The thickness of the asymmetrically notched specimen is $1.0\mathrm{mm}$.

The force-displacement curves with different loading displacement increment obtained by the proposed coupling plastic model are displayed in Fig. 4. The coupled integral governing equations of the coupling plastic damage model are solved using the staggered technique and BFGS. It can be seen that the loading displacement increment $\Delta u$ has significant influence on the reaction forces when using the traditional staggered scheme. The peak force declines gradually with decreasing the loading displacement increment $\Delta u$. Additionally, the reaction force is more likely to rapidly decline in the post-peak region. If the small loading displacement increment $\Delta u=1.0\mathrm{E}{-5}\,\mathrm{mm}$ is used, the converged result may be obtained. Things are different when BFGS is used to solve for the coupled equation. Although the relative bigger loading displacement increment $\Delta u=1.0\mathrm{E}{-3}\,\mathrm{mm}$ is used, the good numerical result can be obtained by the proposed model using BFGS. It is consistent with the previous numerical result calculated by residual controlled staggered algorithm [82]. The damage evolution of the asymmetrically notched specimen is shown in Fig. 3(c). The damage accumulates progressively near the notched tip when the loading displacement is from zero to $u=0.180\mathrm{mm}$. When the loading displacement is $u=0.192\mathrm{mm}$, the diagonal crack across the specimen can be observed, which is consistent with that reported in the references [82,85]. From the above analyses, it demonstrates the effectiveness of the DHPD-FEM plastic model and the solving algorithm of BFGS to address the plastic deformation and fracture process.

### 3.3. The foreign particle erosion-induced failure mechanism in TBCs

#### 3.3.1. Numerical model and material parameters

At last, we investigate the foreign particle erosion-induced failure mechanism of TBCs using the proposed DHPD-FEM plastic model. Since the solid particle erosion mainly damages the surface of ceramic layer (TC layer), the substrate can be generally ignored to reduce the simulation time. The two-dimensional geometric model of APS-TBCs impacted by different particle shapes is shown in Fig. 8. There are three kinds of initial foreign particle erosion configurations shown in Fig. 8(b): ellipsoidal short-axis contact erosion, ellipsoidal long-axis contact erosion and spherical contact erosion [7]. The plane strain problem is assumed in this example.

The left surface of the numerical model is subjected to the symmetry restriction that the displacement in the x-direction is constrained. To guarantee that the nodes on the right surface of TBCs have the same displacement in the horizontal direction, we use the multipoint constraints (MPC) on the right surface of TBCs. Meanwhile, the bottom of the numerical model is constrained in the vertical direction and free in the horizontal direction. The classical penalty function contact algorithm is employed to describe the contact condition between the erosion particle and ceramic top coat. To reduce the computational burden, TC layer is described by dual-horizon peridynamics, meanwhile TGO and BC are described by FEM. The coupling between DHPD and FEM is directly realized by the dual-based coupling approach in Section 2.2. The uniform node-spacing in TC and TGO layers is chosen as $\Delta x=0.5\mathrm{\mu m}$, meanwhile the node-spacing in BC layer is gradually refined from $\Delta x=5\mathrm{\mu m}$ on the bottom surface of BC layer to $\Delta x=0.5\mathrm{\mu m}$ on its upper surface (interface of BC/TGO layer). The peridynamic horizon length and length scale in TC layer are set as $\delta_x=l_s=3\Delta x$. The total simulation time and time step are set as $t=100\mathrm{ns}$ and $\Delta t=0.1\mathrm{ns}$, respectively. The material properties of APS-TBCs are shown in Table 1. The critical energy release rate for TC material is $G_c=0.03\mathrm{N/mm}$. As with the references [7,18], the ideal plastic material constitutive model are assumed in the TGO and BC layers of TBCs. To calibrate the proposed DHPD-FEM plastic model with the erosion experimental data, the bilinear plastic material constitutive model is used in TC layer. The hardening modulus will be calibrated in the following part.

Meanwhile the maximum foreign particle erosion depth is smallest when the particle shape ratio is $a/b=3$. This is because the vertical component of velocity mostly determines the maximum erosion depth [7]. As different shaped particles impact the surface of TC material, different effective contact surfaces in the vertical direction will produce different impact stress magnitudes [7]. For the short-axis contact erosion ($a/b=3$) in Fig. 8(b), the effective contact area is larger than other cases, therefore the effective stress generated in the course of particle erosion is smaller. As a result, the maximum foreign particle erosion depth when the particle shape ratio $a/b=3$ is smaller. Fig. 17(b) illustrates the impact of the foreign particle erosion angle on the erosion rate for different particle shapes. It can be observed that the erosion laws for different particle shapes are totally different and complicated. The erosion rate is comprehensively influenced by the particle shape and erosion angle.

When the particle shape ratio is large ($a/b=3$) for the ellipsoidal short-axis contact erosion, the influence of the foreign particle erosion angle on erosion rate should be considered. It can be intuitively observed from the erosion pits for different particle erosion angles in Fig. 18. The erosion distance in the x direction increases firstly and then decreases with increasing the particle erosion angle. The erosion rate for the particle erosion angle of $\theta=90^{\circ}$ is about seven times as that of $\theta=20^{\circ}$ in Fig. 17(b). When the particle shape ratio is small ($a/b=1/3$) for the ellipsoidal long-axis contact erosion in Fig. 8(b), the erosion pits for different particle erosion angles are shown in Fig. 19. Although the erosion distance in the x direction decreases with the increase of particle erosion angle, the erosion distance in the y direction increases. It makes the particle erosion rate insensitive to the particle erosion angle. The erosion rates for different particle erosion angles are between 700 and 800 in Fig. 17(b). In other words, the impact of the particle erosion angle on the erosion rate can be generally ignored when the particle shape ratio is smaller for the ellipsoidal long-axis contact erosion.

When the foreign particle erosion angle is small ($\theta=20^{\circ}$), the impact of the foreign particle shape on the erosion rate is obvious in Fig. 17(b). The erosion rate increases with decreasing the particle shape ratio $a/b$. Additionally, the erosion rate for ellipsoidal long-axis contact erosion ($a/b=1/3$) is about eight times as that for short-axis contact erosion ($a/b=3$). It can be also intuitively observed from Fig. 20 that the foreign particle shape has great impact on the particle erosion damage morphology of TBCs. The erosion distance in the x direction for the erosion angle of $\theta=20^{\circ}$ increases firstly, and then decreases with the decrease of the particle shape ratio ($a/b$). Meanwhile the erosion distance in the y direction increases with the decrease of the particle shape ratio. When the foreign particle erosion angle is relatively larger ($\theta\geq50^{\circ}$), the influence of particle shape and erosion angle on the erosion rate is finite, the erosion rates are around 700 to 800 for different particle erosion angles and particle shapes in Fig. 16(b). The partial reason can be founded in Fig. 20 that the erosion distance in the x direction for the erosion angle of $\theta=90^{\circ}$ decreases with decreasing the particle shape ratio ($a/b$). However, the erosion distance in the y direction increases with decreasing the particle shape ratio. Therefore, the erosion rate may not be changed significantly.

## 4. Discussions and conclusions

We have presented a coupling plastic damage model of dual-horizon peridynamics and FEM to address the foreign particle erosion failure behaviors of TBCs. It offers a thorough explanation of the coupling plastic damage model along with the evolution law of damage within the framework of bond-based peridynamic phase-field. Additionally, we extend the numerical algorithm of BFGS to solve for the integral coupled equations of damage and deformation in the dual-horizon peridynamic subregions. The foreign particle erosion failure mechanisms of TBCs are comprehensively investigated by the proposed coupling plastic damage model. The numerical results are in quantitative and qualitative agreement with the existing experiment or the previous numerical solutions. It demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed coupling plastic damage model. Additional important remarks are summarized:

(1) The proposed coupling plastic damage model has great potential in dealing with the foreign particle erosion process involving impact and damage of TBCs.

(2) The maximum foreign particle erosion depth increases with decreasing the particle shape ratio $a/b$ when the same particle impact velocity and erosion angle are assumed.

(3) When the foreign particle erosion angle is relatively larger ($\theta\geq50^{\circ}$), the impacts of the foreign particle shape and erosion angle on the erosion rate are finite and can be generally ignored.

(4) When the foreign particle erosion angle is small ($\theta=20^{\circ}$), the impact of the foreign particle shape on the erosion rate is significant. The foreign particle erosion rate increases with decreasing the particle shape ratio $a/b$.

(5) The impact of the foreign particle erosion angle on the erosion rate is significant for the relatively larger particle shape ratio ($a/b=3$). On the contrary, the impact is finite for the relatively smaller particle shape ratio ($a/b=1/3$).

Compared with the existing theoretical models, the advantages of the coupling plastic damage model are summarized:

(1) The damage model can predict and characterize the damage evolution process for particle erosion of TBCs. Therefore, the damage and fragment removal (mass loss) of coating surface observed in the experiment can be well captured and addressed by our damage model.

(2) The spherical particle erosion is commonly used to investigate the particle erosion. Different from that, the particle erosion of arbitrary particle shapes and impingement angles along with the cracking propagation of TBCs can be easily modeled using the proposed damage model.

(3) The stress evolution considering damage (stress concentration and releasing process) in the particle erosion of TBCs can be well captured using our damage model.

Note that the theoretical and numerical models are important in understanding the particle erosion process of TBCs. Additionally, different from the recent numerical models [6,7,9,18], the model proposed in the paper considers the damage and mass loss of TC layer and studies the erosion failure mechanism and erosion resistance combining with experimental data [10]. The proposed coupling plastic damage model is effective to reveal and address the foreign particle erosion process of TBCs involving impact and damage. There is still need for further development of the damage model such as the inclusion of temperature and creep effects of the coating in the model to investigate the evolution of temperature distribution, creep effects and the material parameter change of TBCs in the particle erosion process. Which will take the heating of the substrate and temperature-dependent material parameters of TBCs into account in the future. Additionally, the paper just focuses on APS TBCs, the proposed damage model will be applied to the EB-PVD TBCs in which the modeling and interface damage characterization of the columnar structure will be considered in the future.

## Appendix A. Derivation of the peridynamics-based diffusion micro-modulus

According to the references [86], the classical phase-field (AT2) crack evolution differential equilibrium equation is

$$
G_c l_c\cdot\Delta d+Q_2(\mathbf{x})-Q_1(\mathbf{x})=0 \qquad (A.1)
$$

where the phase-field external driving force is $Q_2(\mathbf{x})=2(1-d)\overline{\mathcal{H}}$. The corresponding internal driving force is $Q_1(\mathbf{x})=G_cd/l_c$.

By comparing the phase-field potentials in the framework of bond-based peridynamics with the traditional phase-field (AT2) model, the peridynamic phase-field micro-modulus can be computed. The nonlocal phase-field potential $\Phi(\mathbf{x})$ at the peridynamic material point $\mathbf{x}$ within the framework of bond-based peridynamics is calculated as

$$
\Phi(\mathbf{x})=1/2\cdot\int_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{x}}}\phi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')\mathrm{d}V_{\mathbf{x}'} \qquad (A.2)
$$

$$
\phi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')=1/2\cdot k^p/|\boldsymbol{\xi}|\cdot(d(\mathbf{x})-d(\mathbf{x}'))^2 \qquad (A.3)
$$

where $\phi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')$ can be viewed as the peridynamics-based micro-diffusive potential of two interaction points.

According to Eq. (A.1), the local phase-field potential $\Phi(\mathbf{x})$ at material point $\mathbf{x}$ related to phase-field gradient in the framework of the traditional phase-field (AT2) model can be written as

$$
\Phi(\mathbf{x})=G_cl_c/2\cdot\nabla d\cdot\nabla d \qquad (A.4)
$$

For a 2D case, the linear phase-field distribution $d(x,y)=x+y$ is assumed. We substitute $d(x,y)=x+y$ into Eqs. (A.2) and (A.4),

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Phi(\mathbf{x})&=1/2\cdot\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\delta_x}1/2\cdot k^p(\xi\cdot\cos\theta+\xi\cdot\sin\theta)^2\mathrm{d}\xi\mathrm{d}\theta\\
&=\pi/2\cdot\int_0^{\delta_x}k^p\cdot\xi^2\mathrm{d}\xi\cdot H=\pi/6\cdot k^p\cdot\delta_x^3\cdot H
\end{aligned} \qquad (A.5)
$$

$$
\Phi(\mathbf{x})=G_cl_s \qquad (A.6)
$$

Using Eqs. (A.3), (A.5) and (A.6), the peridynamics-based diffusion micro-modulus can be obtained as

$$
k^p=6G_cl_s/\left(\pi H\cdot\delta_x^3\right) \qquad (A.7)
$$

Similarly, for a 3D case, the linear phase-field distribution $d(x,y)=x+y+z$ is assumed. We substitute $d(x,y)=x+y+z$ into Eqs. (A.2) and (A.4),

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Phi(\mathbf{x})=&1/2\cdot\int_0^{\delta_x}\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}1/2\cdot k^p(\xi\cdot\cos\theta\sin\varphi+\xi\cdot\sin\theta\sin\varphi+\xi\cdot\cos\varphi)^2\xi\sin\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\xi\mathrm{d}\theta\\
=&\pi\cdot\int_0^{\delta_x}k^p\xi^3\mathrm{d}\xi=\pi k^p\delta_x^4/4
\end{aligned} \qquad (A.8)
$$

$$
\Phi(\mathbf{x})=3G_cl_s/2 \qquad (A.9)
$$

Using Eqs. (A.3), (A.8) and (A.9), the peridynamics-based diffusion micro-modulus is obtained as

$$
k^p=6G_cl_s/\left(\pi\cdot\delta_x^4\right) \qquad (A.10)
$$

## Appendix B. Dynamic wave propagation and static tensile test

We investigate the convergence and efficiency of the proposed coupling model. The examples of the dynamic wave propagation and static tensile test shown in Fig. B.1 are used to validate the proposed coupling model when the damage and plasticity are not considered. The material properties are Young's modulus of $E=1\mathrm{Pa}$, density of $\rho=1\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, Poisson's ratio of $\nu=0$ [63,71,75]. The wave speed is computed as $v=\sqrt{E/\rho}=1\mathrm{m/s}$. It is actually the one-dimensional model calculated and solved using two-dimensional model [63,71,75]. It is frequently employed to validate the convergence and efficiency of numerical methods. The partition of the domain for the FEM and DHPD subregions is illustrated in Fig. B.1. The calculation model of the rectangular plate is discretized with three different material point spacings $\Delta x=0.5\mathrm{mm}$, $\Delta x=0.25\mathrm{mm}$ and $\Delta x=0.125\mathrm{mm}$. $$
\|\mathrm{Error}\|_{L_2}=\frac{\|\mathbf{u}_{num}-\mathbf{u}_{ana}\|}{\|\mathbf{u}_{ana}\|},\ \|\mathbf{u}\|=\left(\int_{\Omega_0}\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega_0\right)^{1/2} \qquad (B.1)
$$

Firstly, we investigate the dynamic wave propagation in the rectangular plate with the fixed time step of $\Delta t=0.5\mathrm{ms}$. The initial displacement fields are set as:

$$
u_0(x,y)=0.0002\exp\left(-(100x)^2\right),\ v_0(x,y)=0,\ x\in[0,0.1],\ y\in[-0.02,0.02] \qquad (B.2)
$$

where $u_0$ and $v_0$ denote the applied displacements in x and y directions, respectively.
