## Abstract

The failure process in a thermal barrier coating (TBC) system can be complicated due to complex microstructure, material property mismatch, high temperature change, chemical reactions during the failure process, etc. It brings considerable difficulties to the modeling technique. A complete failure process in TBC contains multiple failure types, e.g., bulk fracture, delamination of TC (top coating)/TGO (thermally grown oxide) interface and TGO/BC (bond coating) interface, and the interaction among them. However, existing approaches mainly focus on a single failure type and thus are faced with limits on exploring the complete failure mechanism. In this work, we propose a chemo-thermo-mechanical coupled phase field model based on thermodynamic laws, such that the complete TBC fracture process can be captured. The present contribution provides a step forward in comparison with the existing phase field models. Couplings among thermal conduction, material oxidation and damage are considered, and the fundamental differential equations are derived strictly follow the thermodynamic laws. The proposed model is first validated through several benchmarks, and then followed by several simulations of the failure process of a TBC system. The simulations and experiments show that the bulk fracture occurs when the TGO is thin at the cooling process, the growth effect of TGO layer causes damage initiation at the interface and finally transformed into the interfacial delamination, the interaction of interface cracking and bulk material cracking is captured. The proposed model is proven to be a powerful tool in solving the failure process of a TBC system and is promising to handle other similar problems.

## 1. Introduction

Thermal barrier coatings (TBCs) are designed to insulate and protect the super alloy substrate from extreme high temperature. They are usually used to protect the metallic parts used in aircraft engines, gas turbines, or power generation applications [1]. A TBC system in general consists of three layers of different materials (Fig. 1), they are: (a) thermal insulation ceramic coat layer in the top (TC), (b) metallic bond coat (BC) layer in the bottom, and (c) thermally grown oxide (TGO) layer in between. The ceramic coating material can be deposited on a metallic substrate either by means of air-plasma-spray (APS) deposition or electron beam physical-vapor deposition (EB-PVD). Microstructure and interfacial morphology of ceramic coatings are different of these two kinds, and we will limit the scope to APS coatings in this work.

![Fig. 1. Cross-sectional SEM of an air-plasma-sprayed (APS) TBC [2].](fig1.png)

The failure mechanism of a TBC system can be extremely complicated, the most important factors that contribute to the complexity are (a) mismatch of coefficients of thermal expansion (CTEs) among different materials; (b) swelling of TGO layer during oxidation process of BC material; (c) micro-defects (micro-cracks, pores and voids) and (d) the complex microstructure of the interface between TGO and TC. The stress can be considerably high in the vicinity of the interface, and the reason could be twofold: the mismatch of CTEs under the cooling process leads to thermal stresses and the swelling of TGO growth at an elevated temperature leads to the material and stress redistribution [3]. Upon the cooling process, when the TGO layer is thin or absent, the thermal mismatch leads to a tensile stress state at the summit of the TC/TGO interface and a compression stress state at the valley. When the TGO layer becoming thicker, the tensile area may shift to the valley of the interface and the compression area to the summit [4].

To correctly predict the growth of the TGO layer during which the BC material gradually transforms into the TGO material, resulting in the volumetric expansion and change in material properties. A most common and simple approach is to adopt an empirical formula to approximate the relation between the thickness of the TGO layer and time, which is determined experimentally for different cases [5,6]. Another approach is to consider the growth process of the TGO layer through a diffusion and reaction equation [7-9].

The failure process in TBC is rather complicated and the analysis relies heavily on the numerical methods. Early works use thermo-mechanical finite element method (FEM) in combination with stress based failure criteria to study the effect of the residual stress [10-12]. These modeling techniques can successfully predict crack initiation timing and position, but can hardly be used to predict crack propagation which is important in understanding the failure mechanism of a TBC system. Other methods such as virtual crack closure technique (VCCT), extended FEM (XFEM) and cohesive zone method (CZM) are also employed. Bumgardner et al. [13] studied the mechanism of delamination and vertical crack propagation by using in-situ high-temperature digital image correlation (DIC) in combination with VCCT. Other researchers [6,14] used the XFEM to capture the TC and TGO cracking processes, and CZM to capture the interfacial cracking process. To simulate the TC fracture with cohesive elements, Krishnasamy et al. [15] inserted cohesive elements throughout the inter-element boundaries which enables the crack to propagate along an arbitrary direction and path. However, the method is also considered to be mesh dependent. Wang et al. [16] used the peridynamic method to investigate the cooling-induced cracking process of the TC layer. Zhou et al. [8] used a phase field method to simulate the TGO cracking process and cohesive elements to simulate the TC/TGO interfacial delamination, but the interaction between the two failure types was not considered hence the complete failure process cannot be captured which may be less satisfactory in understanding the failure mechanism.

The phase field method has drawn attentions in recent years, it was originated and developed independently from two communities: (a) Landau and Ginzburg formalism in physics community, and (b) the Francfort-Marigo variational formulation of brittle fracture [17] and its regularized version by Bourdin et al. [18,19] in mechanics community [20]. Phase field model has been applied for various problems, such as brittle fracture [21-27], quasi-brittle fracture [28-35], ductile fracture [36-43], dynamic fracture [44-51] and cohesive fracture [52-57], and materials with different properties and microstructures, such as isotropic material [44,51,58], anisotropic material [59-62], porous media [63-65], heterogeneous material [66,67], etc. Phase field model is a smeared crack model, which represents the crack topology using a continuous distributed scalar field, i.e., the phase field. Unlike the discrete crack model (XFEM for example) which defines the displacement jump across the crack surfaces explicitly, the displacement is continuous across the crack in phase field model. Phase field model can predict fracture initiation, propagation, coalescence and branching within one framework, it has eliminated remeshing, tracking the crack trajectory, and the necessity of dealing with the singular stress around the crack tip area which could be troublesome for existing methods. Solving fracture problems using the phase field model can be easily done by solving a system of equation of a multi-field problem and the theory can be implemented into commercial finite element packages to have higher solving efficiency and capability (such as ABAQUS [22,23,68]).

We propose in this paper a chemo-thermo-mechanical coupled phase field model to simulate the complete fracture process of a TBC system. Both the interfacial delamination and the bulk crack process are considered in a unified framework. The couplings among thermal conduction, oxidization, and damage are fully considered. Plastic deformation, high temperature creep effect, and sintering are not considered in this work for simplicity (given that they are minor factor in the failure mechanism studied in this work compared with TGO swelling and mismatch of CTEs [8,13,14,16]). As the crack topology is represented by phase field in a smeared manner, the interface delamination cannot be simulated directly, one can combine a phase field model with interface element model, e.g., cohesive zone model [69], or choose to represent the interface in a smeared manner [70-72] with a modified fracture toughness. We adopted the latter approach in this work and proposed a scalar field variable, i.e. interface phase field, to smear the interface properties and keep the material properties change continuously.

This article is organized as follows. We first consider in Section 2 the basic thermodynamic laws for the continuum body, then the governing equations for each physical field are obtained, the interface treatment is introduced and the modified fracture toughness is obtained using dissipation equivalence. In Section 3 we formulate the finite element discretization for each field. A parametric study is given in Section 4. We provide in Section 5 a few numerical examples to validate the proposed phase field model.

## 2. Thermodynamically consistent phase field model for chemo-thermo-mechanical coupled problem

### 2.1. Phase field model for fracture

To introduce phase field model, we assume a region [ \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{\dim}(\dim=2,3) ] of the solid body with external boundary [ \partial\Omega=[\partial\Omega_u\cup\partial\Omega_t]\subset\mathbb{R}^{\dim-1} ] and internal discontinuity boundary [ \Gamma_c\subset\mathbb{R}^{\dim-1} ] at time [ t ] (consider only mechanical boundaries for simplicity), as shown in Fig. 2a. The external boundary is partitioned into two disjoint parts [ \partial\Omega_u ] and [ \partial\Omega_t ], so that [ \partial\Omega_u\cap\partial\Omega_t=\varnothing ] and [ \partial\Omega_u\cup\partial\Omega_t=\partial\Omega ]. The displacement of a point [ \mathbf{x} ] at time [ t ] is denoted by [ \mathbf{u}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{\dim} ]. Prescribed displacement [ \overline{\mathbf{u}} ] and traction [ \mathbf{t} ] are acting on [ \partial\Omega_u ] and [ \partial\Omega_t ], respectively. The closure of the region is [ \overline{\Omega}\equiv\Omega\cup\partial\Omega\cup\Gamma_c ].

In the discrete fracture model context, the crack's geometry is explicitly represented, which can be achieved by either using a conforming mesh or by adding enrichment functions to the solution space. Phase field model, as a smeared method, approximates the crack topology with a damage zone represented by a scalar field variable [ d\in[0,1] ] (Fig. 2a). The value of [ d ] can be deemed as a measure of damage state of the material, i.e., the material is intact when [ d=0 ], and fully failed when [ d=1 ]. A scalar internal length scale parameter [ l ] is used to control the width of the regularization area. We define the regularized crack surface density functional as [52]

[ I_l(d):=\frac{1}{c_0l}\int_\Omega\left[\alpha(d)+l^2|\nabla d|^2\right]\mathrm{d}V \equiv\int_\Omega\gamma(d,\nabla d)\,\mathrm{d}V, \tag{1} ]

where [ \alpha(d) ] is the crack geometric function which characterizes homogeneous evolution of phase field, [ c_0=4\int_0^1\sqrt{\alpha(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x ] is a scaling parameter ensuring the regularized crack surface approximates the discrete crack surface [ \Gamma_c ], [ \gamma(d,\nabla d) ] is the crack surface density functional.

The crack geometry adopted in this work has the following form [52]

[ \alpha(d)=2d-d^2. \tag{2} ]

And the corresponding scaling parameter [ c_0 ], half-bandwidth of the regularization area [ b ] and the profile of the phase field [ d(x) ] can be obtained

[ c_0=\pi,\qquad b=\frac{\pi}{2}l,\qquad d(x)=1-\sin\left(\frac{|x|}{l}\right). \tag{3} ]

### 2.2. Smeared interface

To introduce the interface phase field method, we first consider a 1D bar with an interface [ \Gamma_{\mathrm{int}} ] as shown in Fig. 2b, the fracture toughness of the interface is [ G_i ]. We introduce an interface phase field variable [ \phi\in(0,1) ] to smear the interface properties over a zone [ \Omega_{\mathrm{int}} ] with a half-bandwidth of [ l_\phi ] (Fig. 2c), the value of [ \phi ] continuously changes from 1 at the middle of the smeared interface to 0 when the distance is equal to [ l_\phi ]. The fracture toughness and strength (on the side of material A for instance) can be written as

[ \begin{cases} G_c(x)=\left[1-(1-\phi(x))^2\right]G_c^i+(1-\phi(x))^2G_c^{\mathrm{A}},\\[4pt] f_t(x)=\left[1-(1-\phi(x))^2\right]f_t^i+(1-\phi(x))^2f_t^{\mathrm{A}}, \end{cases} \tag{4} ]

where [ G_c^{\mathrm{A}} ] and [ G_c^i ] are the fracture toughness of the bulk material and the interface, respectively, [ f_t^i ] and [ f_t^{\mathrm{A}} ] are the interface strength and bulk material strength, respectively.

According to [70,71], a compensation on the equivalent material property should be introduced to guarantee the dissipation equivalence. We have the following relation under the 1D setting

[ G_i=\int_{-b}^{b}\left\{\left[1-(1-\phi)^2\right]\overline{G}_c^i+(1-\phi)^2G_c^{\mathrm{A}}\right\}\gamma\,\mathrm{d}x, \tag{5} ]

where [ \overline{G}_c^i ] is the modified fracture toughness of the interface, the dissipation equivalence ensures the energy release rate of the smeared interface equal to [ G_c^i ] (as illustrated in Fig. 3a), and [ \overline{G}_c^i ] can be obtained by solving Eq. (5) as follows

[ \overline{G}_c^i= \frac{G_i-G_c^{\mathrm{A}}\int_{-b}^{b}(1-\phi)^2\gamma\,\mathrm{d}x} {\int_{-b}^{b}\left[1-(1-\phi)^2\right]\gamma\,\mathrm{d}x}. \tag{6} ]

We choose the following exponential function as the profile of the interface phase field

[ \phi=e^{-\frac{|x|}{l_\phi}}. \tag{7} ]

To avoid the non-physical negative value of [ \overline{G}_c^i ], the following constraint must be satisfied

[ \int_{-b}^{b}(1-\phi)^2\gamma\,\mathrm{d}x<\frac{G_i}{G_c^{\mathrm{A}}}. \tag{8} ]

Using the expressions Eqs. (1), (3) and (7), Eq. (8) can be rewritten as

[ R(r)=\frac{\pi}{2} -\frac{1}{r^3+4r}\left[8-(4r^2+8)e^{-\frac{\pi}{2}r}\right] +\frac{1}{r^3+2r}\left[1-(1+2r^2)e^{-\pi r}\right] <\frac{\pi G_i}{2G_c^{\mathrm{A}}}, \tag{9} ]

with [ r=l/l_\phi ].

The curve of [ \pi G_i/2G_c^{\mathrm{A}} ] vs. [ r ] is shown in Fig. 3b, a smaller ratio of [ G_i/G_c^{\mathrm{A}} ] requires a smaller value of [ r ]. Once values of [ G_i ] and [ G_c^{\mathrm{A}} ] are given, one should choose ratio between [ l ] and [ l_\phi ] properly such that point [ (\log_{10}r,G_i/G_c^{\mathrm{A}}) ] fall in the left side area of the curve to meet the requirement in Eq. (9).

With the definition of Eq. (6), Eq. (4) can be rewritten as follows

[ G_c(x)=\left[1-(1-\phi(x))^2\right]\overline{G}_c^i+(1-\phi(x))^2G_c^{\mathrm{A}}. \tag{10} ]

### 2.3. Kinematics

Restricting the present study to infinitesimal strains, the strain tensor is defined as

[ \boldsymbol{\epsilon}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{u}\nabla\right), \tag{11} ]

with [ \boldsymbol{\epsilon}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{\dim\times\dim} ]. [ \nabla(\cdot) ] and [ (\cdot)\nabla ] are the left and right gradients, respectively. For a chemo-thermo-mechanical coupling problem, the total strain [ \boldsymbol{\epsilon} ] can be divided into elastic strain [ \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_e ], thermal strain [ \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta ] (resulted from thermal expansion) and growth strain [ \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_n ] (resulted from chemical processes), i.e.

[ \boldsymbol{\epsilon}=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_e+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_n, \tag{12} ]

where

[ \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta=\alpha_\theta(\theta-\theta_0)\mathbf{I} \tag{13} ]

and

[ \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_n=\alpha_n n\mathbf{I}, \tag{14} ]

where [ \alpha_\theta ] is the CTE, [ \theta-\theta_0 ] is the temperature change from the reference temperature [ \theta_0 ] to the current temperature [ \theta ], [ \mathbf{I} ] is the second-order identity tensor, [ \alpha_n ] is the swelling coefficient of the TGO, and [ n ] is the volume ratio of TGO [73]. [ n ] measures the extent of oxidation of the BC material, i.e. when: (a) [ n=0 ], the area is composed completely of BC material; (b) [ n\in(0,1) ], the area is undergoing an oxidation process and is mixed with BC and TGO materials; (c) [ n=1 ], the area is completely oxidized and composed solely of TGO material. The time derivative of the total strain is

[ \dot{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}=\dot{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_e+\alpha_\theta\dot{\theta}\mathbf{I}+\alpha_n\dot{n}\mathbf{I}, \tag{15} ]

where [ (\dot{\cdot})=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\cdot) ] for brevity.

### 2.4. Balance equations

#### 2.4.1. Macro-force balance

With the continuum and mechanical boundaries described in Section 2.1, the global balance of linear momentum is specified by

[ \int_\Omega\rho\dot{\mathbf{v}}\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\partial\Omega_t}\mathbf{t}\,\mathrm{d}A + \int_\Omega\mathbf{b}\,\mathrm{d}V, \tag{16} ]

where [ \rho ] is the density, [ \mathbf{v} ] is the velocity. With Cauchy's stress theorem [ \mathbf{t}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{n} ], divergence theorem and considering that the volume [ \mathrm{d}V ] is arbitrary, leading to the Cauchy's first equation of motion in the local form

[ \rho\dot{\mathbf{v}}=\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\mathbf{b}. \tag{17} ]

#### 2.4.2. Micro-force momentum balance

To derive the phase field balance equation, we adopt the micro-force balance law [74-77]. The micro-force momentum balance is given by

[ \int_{\partial\Omega_d}\chi\dot{d}\,\mathrm{d}A - \int_\Omega\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\nabla\dot{d}\,\mathrm{d}V - \int_\Omega\pi\dot{d}\,\mathrm{d}V=0, \tag{18} ]

where [ \chi ] is a scalar micro-traction, [ \pi ] is a scalar micro-stress, [ \boldsymbol{\xi} ] is a vector micro-stress. Using divergence theorem, the above equation can be rewritten as

[ \int_{\partial\Omega_d}(\chi-\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{n})\dot{d}\,\mathrm{d}A + \int_\Omega(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}-\pi)\dot{d}\,\mathrm{d}V=0. \tag{19} ]

Given that [ \dot{d} ] is arbitrary, the above equation can be localized as

[ \chi=\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{n} \tag{20} ]

and

[ \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}-\pi=0. \tag{21} ]

#### 2.4.3. Mass balance

For a specific chemical species (oxygen in this study), the number of molecular per unit volume is described by the concentration [ c ], and the number of molecules diffusing across unit area [ \mathrm{d}A ] per unit time is described by diffusion flux [ \mathbf{j}_n ]. The mass balance in the presence of chemical reaction can be given as

[ \int_\Omega\dot{c}\,\mathrm{d}V = -\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{j}_n\cdot\mathbf{n}\,\mathrm{d}A + \int_\Omega r_n\,\mathrm{d}V, \tag{22} ]

where [ r_n ] is the molecules formation ([ >0 ]) or consumption ([ <0 ]) per unit volume per unit time during the chemical reaction. By using divergence theorem and considering the arbitrariness of the volume [ \mathrm{d}V ], the local mass balance can be obtained as follows

[ \dot{c}=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{j}_n+r_n. \tag{23} ]

### 2.5. Thermodynamic laws

According to the first law of thermodynamics, the following balance equation of energy is given

[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_\Omega\left(w+\frac{1}{2}\rho\dot{\mathbf{v}}^2\right)\mathrm{d}V ={}&\int_\Omega\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{v}\,\mathrm{d}V +\int_{\partial\Omega_t}\mathbf{t}\cdot\mathbf{v}\,\mathrm{d}A +\int_\Omega\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\nabla\dot{d}\,\mathrm{d}V +\int_\Omega\pi\dot{d}\,\mathrm{d}V \\ &-\int_{\partial\Omega_q}\mathbf{j}_q\cdot\mathbf{n}\,\mathrm{d}A +\int_\Omega r_q\,\mathrm{d}V -\int_{\partial\Omega_n}\mu_n\mathbf{j}_n\cdot\mathbf{n}\,\mathrm{d}A, \end{aligned} \tag{24} ]

where [ w ] is the volume density of internal energy, [ r_q ] is the body heat source per unit volume and per unit time, [ \mathbf{j}_q ] is the heat flux per unit area, [ \mu_n\mathbf{j}_n ] is the energy flux while [ \mu_n ] being the chemical potential.

Applying the divergence theorem and considering that the volume is arbitrary, the local form of the balance of energy equation is specified

[ \dot{w}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\dot{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{j}_q +\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\nabla\dot{d} +\pi\dot{d} +r_q +\mu_n\dot{c} -\mu_n r_n -\mathbf{j}_n\cdot\nabla\mu_n. \tag{25} ]

The second law of thermodynamics postulates the total production of entropy [ s ] for all thermodynamic processes is never negative, then we have Clausius-Duhem inequality in the following global form [78]

[ \frac{\mathrm{D}}{\mathrm{D}t}\int_\Omega s\,\mathrm{d}V +\int_{\partial\Omega_q}\frac{\mathbf{j}_q}{\theta}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,\mathrm{d}A -\int_\Omega\frac{r_q}{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}V\ge 0. \tag{26} ]

By virtue of divergence theorem and considering that the integration volume is arbitrary, we have the local form of Clausius-Duhem inequality

[ \theta\dot{s}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{j}_q-\frac{1}{\theta}\mathbf{j}_q\cdot\nabla\theta-r_q\ge 0. \tag{27} ]

By elimination the heat source in above equation with Eq. (25), we have the equivalent form

[ \theta\dot{s} -\frac{1}{\theta}\mathbf{j}_q\cdot\nabla\theta -\dot{w} +\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\dot{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} +\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\nabla\dot{d} +\pi\dot{d} +\mu_n\dot{c} -\mu_n r_n -\mathbf{j}_n\cdot\nabla\mu_n\ge 0. \tag{28} ]

Introducing the Helmholtz free energy per unit volume

[ \psi=w-\theta s, \tag{29} ]

we can rewrite Eq. (28) in the following forms

[ -\frac{1}{\theta}\mathbf{j}_q\cdot\nabla\theta -\dot{\psi} -\dot{\theta}s +\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\dot{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} +\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\nabla\dot{d} +\pi\dot{d} +\mu_n\dot{c} -\mu_n r_n -\mathbf{j}_n\cdot\nabla\mu_n\ge 0. \tag{30} ]

The rate of molecules formation [ r_n ] is related to the rate of oxidation reaction through the following equation according to [8]

[ r_n=-M\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}t}, \tag{31} ]

where [ M ] is the number of moles of oxygen consumed when TGO is formed per unit volume. We assume that the free energy is a scalar function of the state variables [ \{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_e,\theta,d,\nabla d,c\} ], i.e. [ \psi=\psi(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_e,\theta,d,\nabla d,c) ], then

[ \dot{\psi} = \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_e}:\dot{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_e +\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\theta}\dot{\theta} +\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial d}\dot{d} +\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nabla d}\cdot\nabla\dot{d} +\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial c}\dot{c}. \tag{32} ]

Substituting Eqs. (15), (31) and (32) into the dissipation inequality Eq. (30), we have

[ \begin{aligned} &\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_e}\right):\dot{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_e +\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\alpha_\theta\mathbf{I}-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\theta}-s\right)\dot{\theta} +\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nabla d}\right)\cdot\nabla\dot{d} +\left(\pi-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial d}\right)\dot{d} \\ &\quad+\left(\mu_n-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial c}\right)\dot{c} +\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\alpha_n\mathbf{I}+\mu_n M\right)\dot{n} -\frac{1}{\theta}\mathbf{j}_q\cdot\nabla\theta -\mathbf{j}_n\cdot\nabla\mu_n\ge 0. \end{aligned} \tag{33} ]

Assuming that the dissipation rate is independent of [ \{\dot{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_e,\dot{\theta},\nabla\dot{d},\dot{c}\} ], we have the following constitutive equations and dissipation equations:

[ \begin{cases} \boldsymbol{\sigma}=\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_e},\\[6pt] s=\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\alpha_\theta\mathbf{I}-\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial\theta},\\[6pt] \mu_n=\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial c},\\[6pt] \boldsymbol{\xi}=\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nabla d},\\[6pt] \left(\pi-\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial d}\right)\dot{d}\ge 0,\\[6pt] \left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\alpha_n\mathbf{I}+\mu_nM\right)\dot{n}\ge 0,\\[6pt] -\dfrac{1}{\theta}\mathbf{j}_q\cdot\nabla\theta\ge 0,\\[6pt] -\mathbf{j}_n\cdot\nabla\mu_n\ge 0. \end{cases} \tag{34} ]

Assuming that the free energy can be decomposed into four parts, elastic energy [ \psi_e ], fracture energy [ \psi_d ], thermal energy [ \psi_\theta ], chemical energy related to diffusion [ \psi_c ],

[ \psi=\psi_e(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_e,d)+\psi_\theta(\theta,d)+\psi_d(d,\nabla d)+\psi_c(c). \tag{35} ]

#### 2.5.1. Linear constitutive relations

For hyperelastic materials, the initial elastic energy density can be expressed as

[ \psi_{e0}=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_e:\mathbb{C}:\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_e, \tag{36} ]

where [ \mathbb{C}=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_e} ] is the elastic tensor. Considering that the fracture process acts only to the tensile part, the stored energy density is given [79]

[ \psi_e(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_e,d)=\omega(d)\psi_0^++\psi_0^-, \tag{37} ]

where [ \omega(d) ] is the degradation function. The positive part [ \psi_0^+ ] due to tension and negative part [ \psi_0^- ] due to compression can be defined as

[ \psi_0^+=\frac{1}{2}\lambda\left\langle\mathrm{tr}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_e\right\rangle_+^2+\mu\mathrm{tr}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_e\right)_+^2\right),\qquad \psi_0^-=\frac{1}{2}\lambda\left\langle\mathrm{tr}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_e\right\rangle_-^2+\mu\mathrm{tr}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_e\right)_-^2\right), \tag{38} ]

where [ \lambda ] and [ \mu ] are the Lamé constants, [ \langle x\rangle_\pm=(x\pm|x|)/2 ] is the Macaulay bracket, and [ \left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_e\right)_\pm=\sum_{i=1}^{\dim}\langle\varepsilon_i\rangle_\pm\mathbf{n}_i\otimes\mathbf{n}_i ], [ \{\varepsilon_i\}_{i=1,\dots,\dim} ] are the principal strains and [ \{\mathbf{n}_i\}_{i=1,\dots,\dim} ] are their eigenvectors.

Applying the degradation to the tensile part of the energy and considering Eq. (34)[ _1 ], we have

[ \boldsymbol{\sigma}=\omega(d)\frac{\partial\psi_0^+}{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_e}+\frac{\partial\psi_0^-}{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_e}. \tag{39} ]

By applying the degradation function to the tensile part, this model can prevent unrealistic damage evolving under compression. We adopt the hybrid formulation proposed by Ambati et al. [20] during calculation, which simplifies the calculation for stress without losing accuracy with [ \boldsymbol{\sigma}=\omega(d)\frac{\partial\psi_0}{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_e} ] instead of Eq. (39).

#### 2.5.2. Evolution equation

Assuming that each individual dissipation process decouples with each other (One can also establish a more sophisticated model by considering the cross-coupling effect between different dissipative processes [80]), it can be derived from the laws of thermodynamics that

[ \begin{cases} -\dfrac{1}{\theta}\mathbf{j}_q\cdot\nabla\theta\ge 0,\\[6pt] -\mathbf{j}_n\cdot\nabla\mu_n\ge 0,\\[6pt] \left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\alpha_n\mathbf{I}+\mu_nM\right)\dot{n}\ge 0,\\[6pt] \left(\pi-\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial d}\right)\dot{d}\ge 0. \end{cases} \tag{40} ]

To meet the dissipation requirement of Eq. (40)[ _1 ], the Fourier's law for the thermal dissipation is adopted

[ \mathbf{j}_q=-\boldsymbol{\kappa}\cdot\nabla\theta, \tag{41} ]

where [ \boldsymbol{\kappa} ] is the thermal conductivity tensor, and will reduce to a scalar thermal conductivity [ \kappa ] for isotropic materials in this paper. Following the treatment similar to [75,81], and considering the degradation on thermal conductivity [ \kappa ], we have temperature governing equation (More details can be found in Appendix A)

[ \rho C\dot{\theta}=\omega(d)\kappa\Delta\theta+r_q, \tag{42} ]

where [ C ] is the specific heat capacity. To meet the dissipation requirement of Eq. (40)[ _2 ], the Fick's law is adopted for diffusion dissipation

[ \mathbf{j}_n=-m\nabla\mu_n, \tag{43} ]

where [ m ] is a positive parameter. The chemical potential [ \mu_n ] can be calculated with the following relation [8]

[ \mu_n=\mathcal{N}c, \tag{44} ]

where [ \mathcal{N} ] is the chemical modulus, inserting Eqs. (31) and (44) into Eq. (23), we have reaction-diffusion equation

[ \dot{c}=D\Delta c-M\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}t}, \tag{45} ]

where [ D=m\mathcal{N} ] is the diffusion coefficient. To meet the dissipation requirement of Eq. (40)[ _3 ], we can assume the TGO growth rate as

[ \dot{n}=k_0\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\alpha_n\mathbf{I}+\mu_nM\right), \tag{46} ]

where [ k_0 ] is a positive parameter, and we use [ k_1=k_0(1-n) ] from [8] to limit the growth rate of TGO. Ignoring the strain term, and consider the degradation on [ k_1 ], we have

[ \dot{n}=\omega(d)G(1-n)c, \tag{47} ]

where [ G=k_1\mathcal{N}M ].

With the definition of crack surface density in Eq. (1). The fracture energy density can be given as

[ \psi_d=G_c\gamma. \tag{48} ]

The micro-stress [ \boldsymbol{\xi} ] and [ \pi ] can be obtained by substituting Eq. (48) into Eq. (34)[ _4 ] and (34)[ _5 ] as

[ \boldsymbol{\xi}=\frac{2G_cl}{c_0}\nabla d,\qquad \pi=\omega'(d)\psi_0^++\frac{\partial\psi_0}{\partial d}+\frac{G_c}{c_0l}\alpha'(d)+\eta\dot{d}, \tag{49} ]

where [ \eta ] is a positive parameter. Neglecting the temperature energy term and substituting (49) into Eq. (21) yields the governing equation of phase field

[ Y-\left(G_c\delta_d\gamma+\eta\dot{d}\right)=0, \tag{50} ]

where [ Y=-\omega'(d)\psi_0^+ ] is the elastic energy driving force, [ \delta_d\gamma=\frac{1}{c_0l}\left(\alpha'-2l^2\Delta d\right) ] is the functional derivative of crack surface density [ \gamma ]. The above equation can be rewritten as Kuhn-Tucker loading/unloading conditions

[ \dot{d}\ge 0,\qquad Y-\left(G_c\delta_d\gamma+\eta\dot{d}\right)\le 0,\qquad \left[Y-\left(G_c\delta_d\gamma+\eta\dot{d}\right)\right]\dot{d}\equiv 0. \tag{51} ]

To prevent the crack from self-healing and guarantee the irreversibility condition [ (\dot{d}\ge 0) ], we adopt a history maximum energy density [ \mathcal{H} ] to replace [ \psi_0^+ ] in the energetic driving force [ Y ] [82]. The expression of [ \mathcal{H} ] is given as

[ \mathcal{H}=\max_{\tau\in[0,t]}\left\{\psi_0^+,\frac{f_t^2}{2E}\right\}, \tag{52} ]

where [ f_t ] is the tensile strength and [ E ] is the Young's modulus.

Following the work of Wu [52], a specific cohesive laws can be approximated by properly elaborate the following degradation function [ \omega(d) ]

[ \omega(d)=\frac{(1-d)^p}{(1-d)^p+Q(d)},\qquad Q(d)=a_1d\left[1+a_2d\left(1+a_3d(\cdots)\right)\right], \tag{53} ]

where [ p>0 ] is the exponent and [ a_i ] are coefficients that determined by the desired traction separation law. We adopt a Cornelissen softening relationship [83] in this work by adopting the degradation function with the following parameters

[ p=2,\qquad a_1=\frac{4EG_c}{\pi f_t^2},\qquad a_2=1.3868,\qquad a_3=0.6567. \tag{54} ]

## 3. Discretization

In this section, the spatial and temporal discretization of the problem is introduced. The whole problem's calculation can be divided into two parts, the first part is the calculation of multi-field coupling problem, which is a transient state problem and both spatial and temporal discretization are needed. The second is the calculation of the interface phase field, which is a steady state problem and only spatial discretization is needed.

### 3.1. Chemo-thermo-mechanical coupled problem

#### 3.1.1. Spatial discretization

We give here directly the finite element equations for the proposed model, the strong and weak form of the governing equations can be found in Appendix B. The field variables can be discretized as follows

[ \mathbf{u}=\sum_i[N_u]^T\{\mathbf{u}\}^i,\quad \theta=\sum_i[N_\theta]^T\{\theta\}^i,\quad d=\sum_i[N_d]^T\{d\}^i,\quad c=\sum_i[N_c]^T\{c\}^i, \tag{55} ]

[ \boldsymbol{\epsilon}=\sum_i[B_u]^T\{\mathbf{u}\}^i,\quad \nabla\theta=\sum_i[B_\theta]^T\{\theta\}^i,\quad \nabla d=\sum_i[B_d]^T\{d\}^i,\quad \nabla c=\sum_i[B_c]^T\{c\}^i, ]

where [ \{\cdot\}^i ] denotes the corresponding nodal vectors for element [ i ], [ [\cdot]^T ] is the transpose of a matrix, [ [N_u] ], [ [N_\theta] ], [ [N_d] ], [ [N_c] ] are the shape function matrices for displacement, temperature, phase field and oxygen concentration, respectively, and [ [B_u] ], [ [B_\theta] ], [ [B_d] ], [ [B_c] ] are their derivatives.

The governing equations of each field are specified as follows. For displacement field

[ [M]\{\ddot{\mathbf{u}}\}=\{F_u^{\mathrm{ext}}\}-\{F_u^{\mathrm{int}}\}, \tag{56} ]

where

[ [M]=\sum_i\int_{\Omega^i}\rho[N_u]^T[N_u]\,\mathrm{d}V, \tag{57} ]

[ \{F_u^{\mathrm{int}}\}=\sum_i\int_{\Omega^i}\omega(d)[B_u]^T\{\sigma\}\,\mathrm{d}V =\sum_i\int_{\Omega^i}\omega(d)[B_u]^T[D]\left(\{\epsilon\}-\{\epsilon_\theta\}-\{\epsilon_n\}\right)\mathrm{d}V, \tag{58} ]

[ \{F_u^{\mathrm{ext}}\}=\sum_i\left(\int_{\Omega^i}[N_u]^T\{\mathbf{b}\}\,\mathrm{d}V+\int_{\partial\Omega_t^i}[N_u]^T\{\mathbf{t}\}\,\mathrm{d}A\right), \tag{59} ]

with [ [M] ] being the mass matrix, [ \{F_u^{\mathrm{int}}\} ] and [ \{F_u^{\mathrm{ext}}\} ] are the internal force vector and external force vector, respectively, [ [D] ] is the material elastic matrix, [ \{\mathbf{b}\} ] and [ \{\mathbf{t}\} ] are the nodal body force vector and traction vector, respectively, [ \{\epsilon\} ], [ \{\epsilon_\theta\} ] and [ \{\epsilon_n\} ] are the total strain, thermal strain and growth strain vector, respectively.

For temperature field

[ [C]\{\dot{\theta}\}=\{F_\theta^{\mathrm{ext}}\}-\{F_\theta^{\mathrm{int}}\}, \tag{60} ]

where

[ [C]=\sum_i\int_{\Omega^i}\rho C[N_\theta]^T[N_\theta]\,\mathrm{d}V, \tag{61} ]

[ \{F_\theta^{\mathrm{int}}\}=\sum_i\int_{\Omega^i}\omega(d)\kappa[B_\theta]^T[B_\theta]\{\theta\}\,\mathrm{d}V, \tag{62} ]

[ \{F_\theta^{\mathrm{ext}}\}=\sum_i\left(\int_{\Omega^i}[N_\theta]^T\{r_q\}\,\mathrm{d}V+\int_{\partial\Omega_q^i}[N_\theta]^T\{\mathbf{j}_q\}\,\mathrm{d}A\right), \tag{63} ]

with [ [C] ] being the thermal capacitance matrix, [ \{F_\theta^{\mathrm{int}}\} ] and [ \{F_\theta^{\mathrm{ext}}\} ] are the internal and external thermal power vector, respectively, [ \{r_q\} ] and [ \{\mathbf{j}_q\} ] are the heat source intensity vector and external heat flux vector respectively.

For phase field

[ [H]\{\dot{d}\}=\{Y\}-\{F_d\}, \tag{64} ]

where

[ [H]=\sum_i\int_{\Omega^i}\eta[N_d]^T[N_d]\,\mathrm{d}V, \tag{65} ]

[ \{Y\}=\sum_i\int_{\Omega^i}\omega'(d)[N_d]^T[N_d]\mathcal{H}\,\mathrm{d}V, \tag{66} ]

[ \{F_d\}=\sum_i\left(\int_{\Omega^i}\frac{G_c}{c_0l}\alpha'[N_d]^T[N_d]\,\mathrm{d}V +\int_{\Omega^i}\frac{2G_cl}{c_0}[B_d]^T[B_d]\{d\}\,\mathrm{d}V\right), \tag{67} ]

with [ [H] ] being the artificial viscosity matrix, [ \{Y\} ] is the energetic driving force vector, [ \{F_d\} ] is the internal fracture energy vector.

For the oxygen concentration field

[ [\dot{c}]=\{F_c^{\mathrm{diffusion}}\}-\{F_c^{\mathrm{reaction}}\}, \tag{68} ]

where

[ \{F_c^{\mathrm{diffusion}}\}=\sum_i\left(\int_{\Omega^i}D[B_c]^T[B_c]\{c\}\,\mathrm{d}V +\int_{\partial\Omega_n^i}[N_c]^T\{\mathbf{j}_n\}\,\mathrm{d}A\right), \tag{69} ]

[ \{F_c^{\mathrm{reaction}}\}=\sum_i\left(-\int_{\Omega^i}\omega(d)MG(1-n)[N_c]^T\{c\}\,\mathrm{d}V\right), \tag{70} ]

with [ \{F_c^{\mathrm{diffusion}}\} ] is the total diffusion flux vector, [ \{F_c^{\mathrm{reaction}}\} ] is the formation or consumption vector due to chemical reaction, [ \{\mathbf{j}_n\} ] is the external diffusion flux vector.

#### 3.1.2. Temporal discretization

For a time span [ [0,T] ], it is divided into [ N-1 ] time intervals [ [t_n,t_{n+1}] ] [ (n=1,2,\ldots,N-1) ], [ t_1=0 ] is the initial moment and [ \Delta t_n=t_{n+1}-t_n ] is the [ i ]th interval length. For a time dependent variable [ x ], we use [ x_{n-1},x_n ] and [ x_{n+1} ] to denote [ x(t_{n-1}) ], [ x(t_n) ] and [ x(t_{n+1}) ], respectively. Applying the central difference method to [ \{\ddot{\mathbf{u}}\} ], we have

[ \{\ddot{\mathbf{u}}\}_n=\frac{1}{\Delta t_n^2}\left(\{\mathbf{u}\}_{n-1}-2\{\mathbf{u}\}_n+\{\mathbf{u}\}_{n+1}\right). \tag{71} ]

and the forward difference method to [ \{\dot{\theta}\} ], [ \{\dot{d}\} ] and [ \{\dot{c}\} ], we have

[ \begin{cases} \{\dot{\theta}\}_n=\dfrac{\{\theta\}_{n+1}-\{\theta\}_n}{\Delta t_n},\\[6pt] \{\dot{d}\}_n=\dfrac{\{d\}_{n+1}-\{d\}_n}{\Delta t_n},\\[6pt] \{\dot{c}\}_n=\dfrac{\{c\}_{n+1}-\{c\}_n}{\Delta t_n}. \end{cases} \tag{72} ]

Solutions for the moment [ t_{n+1} ] can be obtained as follows

[ \begin{aligned} \{\mathbf{u}\}_{n+1} &=[M]^{-1}\left[\Delta t_n^2\left(\{F_u^{\mathrm{ext}}\}_n-\{F_u^{\mathrm{int}}\}_n\right)-[M]\{\mathbf{u}\}_{n-1}+2[M]\{\mathbf{u}\}_n\right],\\ \{\theta\}_{n+1} &=[C]^{-1}\left[\Delta t_n\left(\{F_\theta^{\mathrm{ext}}\}_n-\{F_\theta^{\mathrm{int}}\}_n\right)+[C]\{\theta\}_n\right],\\ \{d\}_{n+1} &=[H]^{-1}\left[\Delta t_n\left(\{Y\}_n-\{F_d\}_n\right)+[H]\{d\}_n\right],\\ \{c\}_{n+1} &=[I]^{-1}\left[\Delta t_n\left(\{F_c^{\mathrm{diffusion}}\}_n-\{F_c^{\mathrm{reaction}}\}_n\right)+[I]\{c\}_n\right]. \end{aligned} \tag{75} ]

By adopting the lumped form of matrices [ [M] ], [ [C] ] and [ [H] ], Eqs. (75) can be solved without the need of factorizing a matrix [84], thus accelerates the calculation. The explicit scheme is conditionally stable, the numerical stability limits of displacement field, temperature field, phase field and concentration field are [85,86]

[ \begin{cases} \Delta t_n\approx\dfrac{h_{\min}}{c_d},\qquad c_d=\sqrt{\dfrac{\lambda+2\mu}{\rho}},\\[8pt] \Delta t_\theta\approx\dfrac{\rho Ch_n^2}{2\kappa},\\[8pt] \Delta t_d\approx\dfrac{\eta h_{\min}^2}{2lG_c},\\[8pt] \Delta t_c\approx\dfrac{h_{\min}^2}{2D}, \end{cases} \tag{77} ]

respectively, where [ h_{\min} ] is the smallest distance between two nodes in one element. The time increment adopted in the simulation is

[ \Delta t=\beta\cdot\min\{\Delta t_u,\Delta t_\theta,\Delta t_d,\Delta t_c\}, \tag{78} ]

where a parameter [ \beta\in(0,1) ] is used to ensure the satisfaction of the conditions for numerical stability, and [ \beta=0.5 ] is adopted in this work.

### 3.2. Interface phase field problem

The interface phase field variable can be discretized as follows

[ \phi=\sum_i[N_\phi]^T\{\phi\}^i,\qquad \nabla\phi=\sum_i[B_\phi]^T\{\phi\}^i, \tag{79} ]

[ [N_\phi] ] is the shape function matrix for interface phase field, and [ [B_\phi] ] is the derivative of the shape function, [ \{\phi\} ] is the interface phase field vector. The Newton-Raphson algorithm is used for the calculation. In a certain incremental step, the iteration scheme can be written as

[ \{\phi\}_{l+1}=\{\phi\}_l-[K_\phi]_l^{-1}\{r_\phi\}_l, \tag{80} ]

where the subscript [ l ] represents the index of the iteration number, [ [\cdot]^{-1} ] denotes the inverse of a matrix, residual vector [ \{r_\phi\} ] and tangential stiffness matrices [ [K_\phi] ] can be calculated through the following equations

[ \begin{aligned} \{r_\phi\}&=\sum_i\left(\int_{\Omega^i}\frac{1}{c_0l_\phi}\alpha'[N_\phi]^T[N_\phi]\,\mathrm{d}V +\int_{\Omega^i}\frac{2l_\phi}{c_0}[B_\phi]^T[B_\phi]\{\phi\}\,\mathrm{d}V\right),\\ [K_\phi]&=\sum_i\left(\int_{\Omega^i}\frac{1}{c_0l_\phi}\alpha''[N_\phi]^T[N_\phi]\,\mathrm{d}V +\int_{\Omega^i}\frac{2l_\phi}{c_0}[B_\phi]^T[B_\phi]\,\mathrm{d}V\right). \end{aligned} \tag{81} ]

In particular, the present work neglects the updating of the interface's position and property changes, which thus has made the problem be linear, the equation can be solved by one time and the iteration can be escaped.

### 5.4. Top coating crack

We take a one-period model for simulation, the wave length of the interface roughness is [ L=30\,\mu\mathrm{m} ], the amplitude is [ A=5\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] and the boundary conditions are the same with that in Fig. 10a. The initial thickness of the TGO layer is [ 0\,\mu\mathrm{m} ]. The temperature drops from the initial value of [ 1150^\circ\mathrm{C} ] to [ 20^\circ\mathrm{C} ] in [ 30\,\mathrm{min} ]. Following the work of Zhou et al. [8], we chose the oxygen diffusion coefficients of TBC and BC to be equal for simplicity. We do not consider the oxygen diffusion in TC layer, and a prescribed oxygen concentration is given on TC/TGO interface with a value of [ 1.55\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}} ] [73]. A finer mesh density is assigned to the critical zone near the interface with a mesh size of [ h=0.0002\,\mathrm{mm} ], and the internal length scale of phase field is [ l=0.0005\,\mathrm{mm} ].

The simulation results and experimental observation from [90] are shown in Fig. 13. The cracks are initiated at the peak of the TC/TGO interface due to the mismatch of CTEs, and then propagate along the interface for a short distance, then propagate horizontally at the off-peak position of the interface, until cracks coalesce with each other at the TC layer, and leading to the ultimate spallation failure of the TBC. Furthermore, the horizontally propagated cracks may interact with pre-existing defects (the micro-cracks, pores and voids), facilitates the spallation of the coating. The results are quite different with that of Section 5.3, which can be explained by the difference of TGO thickness. The thickness of TGO layer in Section 5.3 is fixed at [ 5\,\mu\mathrm{m} ], while the thickness of the current simulation is nearly [ 0\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] (we define TGO material by volume ratio [ n>0.8 ] in this paper), which clarifies that the interfacial delamination of BC/TGO interface is caused by the mismatch of CTEs between TGO and BC material.

## 6. Conclusion

In this work, a chemo-thermo-mechanical coupled phase field framework is proposed. Interfacial properties are smeared for the calculation of the interfacial delamination, several numerical simulations are conducted and experimental observations are offered for comparison. The following conclusion can be obtained:

The proposed model can simulate both interfacial and bulk cracking of the TBC system, the BC/TGO interface delamination are observed in the cooling process when the TGO thickness is non-zero. When the TGO thickness is zero or very small, the interface crack appears at the peak of TC/TGO interface and propagated along the interface for a short distance, then transformed into the horizontally propagated crack inside TC layer and coalesces with another crack.

The swelling of the TGO layer causes the damage initiation at the TC/TGO interface, and formed into cracks at the cooling period, eventually leading to the delamination of the TC/TBC interface. The cracks at the TC/TGO interface and the cracks formed at the BC/TGO interface are interact with each other and may develop further into bulk cracks of the TGO layer.

The dissipation equivalence of the interface fracture is guaranteed by adopting a modified fracture toughness and validated though a numerical simulation. To prevent the non-physical negative value of the modified fracture toughness, the choice of interface phase field half-bandwidth has been discussed.

The proposed model is a powerful and effective tool for TBC failure simulation and other multi-field problems calculation. Still, there is much room for this model to improve, current model can predict only the cracks caused by tensile stress, different failure criteria can be considered in this model to improve the performance. Besides, the micro-defects in TBC system can also be considered in the future work.

### 1. Strong form of the governing equations

The strong form of governing equations are summarized as follows

[ \begin{cases} \nu_u=\rho\dot{\mathbf{v}}-\omega(d)\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\mathbf{b}=0,\\[6pt] \nu_\theta=\rho C\dot{\theta}-\omega(d)\kappa\Delta\theta-r_q=0,\\[6pt] \nu_d=Y-\left(G_c\delta_d\gamma+\eta\dot{d}\right)=0,\\[6pt] \nu_c=\dot{c}-D\Delta c+M\dfrac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}t}=0, \end{cases} \tag{91} ]

and the boundaries corresponding to Dirichlet and Neumann type boundary conditions, we have

[ \begin{cases} \partial\Omega_u\cup\partial\Omega_t=\partial\Omega,\qquad \partial\Omega_u\cap\partial\Omega_t=\varnothing,\\ \partial\Omega_\theta\cup\partial\Omega_q=\partial\Omega,\qquad \partial\Omega_\theta\cap\partial\Omega_q=\varnothing,\\ \partial\Omega_c\cup\partial\Omega_n=\partial\Omega,\qquad \partial\Omega_c\cap\partial\Omega_n=\varnothing, \end{cases} \tag{92} ]

### 2. Weak form of the governing equations

The weak form is constructed by multiplying each equation by an appropriate arbitrary test function, integrating over the domain of the problem, as follows
