## ABSTRACT

This work develops an integrated theoretical-experimental approach to investigate the effect of TC porosity and TC/TGO interface roughness on oxidation-induced failure in APS thermal barrier coating system (TBCs) under long-term isothermal exposure. A chemo-mechanical-damage coupled framework is proposed, integrating entire analyses of oxygen transport in porous TC, TGO formation, stress evolution, and crack initiation/propagation. Experimental and numerical results show that TC porosity and interface roughness jointly regulate interfacial oxidation behavior. TC porosity below [ 4\% ] significantly reduces interfacial oxidation rate by impeding oxygen diffusion, thereby delaying crack initiation and propagation at the TC/TGO interface. Interface with amplitude exceeding [ 15\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] introduces complex stress distributions, increasing TGO thickness non-uniformity and driving crack paths to deviate from the TC/TGO interface, which effectively prolongs TC spallation time. This study establishes microstructure-property relationships for TBCs, providing a direct guide for evaluating TBCs reliability in high-temperature environments.

## 1. Introduction

Thermal barrier coatings (TBCs) are critical for protecting Ni-based superalloys used in gas turbine components like blades, nozzles, and combustor liners [1-4]. Typically consisting of an yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) top coat (TC) layer, a bond coat (BC) layer, and a thermally grown oxide (TGO) layer, TBCs reduce metal temperatures and extend component life by preventing oxidation, creep, and thermal cycling fatigue [5-8]. This protection is essential for increasing turbine inlet temperatures and improving engine efficiency. However, enhancing TBC durability and integrity remains a key challenge for further improving gas turbine economics. TBC damage reduces thermal insulation, leading to elevated metal temperatures and eventual component failure due to thermomechanical stress concentration. Consequently, understanding and mitigating TBC damage processes are central to gas turbine research and engineering.

Several mechanisms contribute to TBC damage, including erosion (infiltration of calcium-magnesium aluminosilicates), TGO growth, foreign object damage (FOD), and sintering/phase transformation within the TC [9-12]. However, TC spallation driven by TGO formation remains the predominant failure mode. Typically, an MCrAlY [ (\mathrm{M} = \mathrm{Ni},\mathrm{Co}) ] is employed as the BC layer, and the TGO forms at the TC/BC interface due to BC oxidation at high temperatures. A key issue is the volumetric expansion associated with BC oxidation during TGO formation. This expansion is constrained by the surrounding TC and BC, generating immense compressive growth stress (on the order of GPa) within the newly formed TGO [13-20]. This accumulated stress drives failure in two primary ways. First, the strain energy in the oxide generates a driving force for delamination of the TGO and overlying TC layer. Second, compressive stress is developed in the TGO layer as it tries to expand but is restricted. The compressive stresses in the oxide must be balanced by tensile stresses in the TC layer. These tensile stresses can generate cracks in the TC layer and TC layer spallation takes place consequently.

The oxidation behavior and evolution of TGO are highly complex due to diverse reaction mechanisms and have been extensively studied. TGO growth kinetics depend on several factors, prompting the development of mathematical models—often empirically based and incorporating these factors—to describe both growth kinetics and associated stresses [21,22]. Hille et al. modelled TGO growth by combining oxygen diffusion with the oxidation reaction, neglecting the top coat's (TC) influence on oxygen diffusion [23]. They described oxidation as TGO progressively consuming the bond coat (BC), with a mixed, partially oxidized region near the TGO/BC interface. Recognizing that oxygen-aluminium interdiffusion also affects growth, Osorio et al. proposed an analytical TGO growth model [24]. Building on diffusion principles, Gupta and co-workers developed a model incorporating the actual TC/BC interface topography to assess TBC lifespan [25]. More recently, Suo et al. established a coupling diffusion-reaction-mechanics model investigating BC oxidation, including hydrostatic stress effects on oxygen chemical potential [26]. Shen et al. introduced a chemo-mechanically-coupled constitutive theory to account for stress-diffusion interactions, enabling the study of TBC oxidation under varying coupling coefficients [19,27]. These models enable prediction of TGO thickness and growth rate over time, crucial for evaluating TBC long-term performance and durability.

While the detrimental effect of TGO formation is widely recognized, quantitative assessments of oxidation-induced failure within the oxide and surrounding ceramic remain scarce. Ranjbar-Far et al. [10] and Busso et al. [11] observed that tensile stresses developing in peak regions of BC layer and valley regions of TC layer, potentially inducing crack initiation and propagation. Consequently, crack propagation in TGO-induced failure has been extensively studied. Ranjbar-Far et al. [13,14] studied interface crack propagation along the TC/TGO interface using a critical normal stress criterion, finding crack occurrence dependent on interface morphology and TGO thickness. Similarly, Baker et al. [15] investigated interface crack initiation and propagation, noting a tendency for cracks to propagate towards the TC/TGO interface, demonstrating the key role of TGO morphology and deformation in crack nucleation and growth. Evans et al. [18] observed horizontal cracks forming in TC valley regions after 80 cycles, subsequently extending along the TC/TGO interface and coalescing, ultimately causing TBC spallation. Padture et al. [19] identified three common TGO wrinkle-related failure modes and proposed potential failure reasons. Further demonstrating failure mechanisms, Shen et al. [17] conducted thermal cycling test, identifying element diffusion and crack evolution as primary contributors to TBC failure. To inhibit the damage behavior of TBCs, regulating the growth of interfacial oxides and stress evolution through the design of new material systems has become a research frontier [3,28]. Collectively, these studies reveal key cracking behaviours associated with interface oxidation reactions.

Despite these advances, experimental observations of TGO-induced oxidation damage accumulation near the TC/TGO interface remain poorly captured, and the correlation between damage patterns and microstructure is underexplored. Accurately simulating the complex, unified process of crack initiation and propagation—which may simultaneously involve chemical reaction-induced material changes, dynamic element migration/diffusion, and TC/TGO interface delamination—remains challenging. Crucially, the interaction between TGO growth stress and TBC microstructure significantly governs fracture behavior. For instance, TC porosity variations substantially impact TGO growth rate and TBC lifespan [30], as oxygen transport within the TC is porosity-dependent and critically influences TGO development [6,25,31]. Furthermore, interfacial roughness markedly affects fracture mode; increased roughness enhances the TC/BC contact area, potentially leading to tortuous crack paths [26]. Shot peening process can improve the planeness of interface, therefore improve isothermal oxidation resistance and high thermal shock life in TBCs [29]. Unlike aero-engine applications, gas turbine TBCs endure long-term high-temperature service, subjecting the TGO to prolonged oxidation. Previous research often focused on thermal cycling effects, assuming strain relaxation at high temperatures with thermal mismatch stress and cracking occurring during cooling [3,32], leaving high-temperature damage origins comparatively unexplored. Consequently, this study investigates the effects of porosity and interface roughness on oxidation failure during prolonged high-temperature exposure.

This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes a chemomechanical-damage modelling framework and implements comprehensive Multiphysics simulations to predict oxidation-induced crack patterns. Section 3 presents long-term experimental validation of these numerical results. Section 4 comparatively analyses numerical and experimental findings, specifically examining porosity- and interface-roughness-dependent crack propagation mechanisms in TBCs. The study concludes with key findings summarized in Section 5.

## 2. Theoretical and computational framework for chemo-mechanical-damage modelling

In this section, we present a chemo-mechanical-damage modelling framework to elucidate the mechanisms by which the porosity of the TC layer governs the oxidation kinetics of the TGO and induces oxidation-driven failure in air plasma sprayed (APS) TBCs. The framework integrates oxygen diffusion, TGO growth dynamics, stress evolution, and crack propagation mechanics (Fig. 1). The APS TBCs is modelled as a multi-layered structure consisting of a porous yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) top coat deposited on a metallic substrate (SUB), with a bond coat (BC) interlayer serving as a transitional zone. The inherent porous morphology arising from the APS manufacturing process enables oxygen ingress through the TC layer during high-temperature service. The diffusion kinetics are strongly modulated by the TC porosity, which dictates the oxygen concentration gradient profile across the TBC system. The TC layer is treated as a homogenized medium with effective transport properties that account for its underlying microstructural porosity.

### 2.1. Dynamic growth model of TGO

#### 2.1.1. Oxygen diffusion kinetics in TBCs

During the high-temperature oxidation process, oxygen species from the combustion environment permeate through the porous TC layer via complex transport pathways and react with aluminum species diffusing outward from the BC layer at the TC/BC interface, resulting in the progressive formation and thickening of the TGO layer. A critical challenge in accurately predicting TBCs lifetime lies in resolving the multimodal oxygen transport mechanisms within the heterogeneous TC microstructure, which is characterized by interconnected intra-splat pores and inter-splat microcracks. Therefore, we seek to deconvolve the dominant gas diffusion mechanisms operative within the porous TC layer. The TC is typically treated as a homogenized medium with effective transport properties that integrate the influence of its underlying porosity. Gas diffusion through such disordered porous media may occur via three primary mechanisms—molecular diffusion, viscous flow, and Knudsen diffusion—each governed by distinct physical principles and dictated by the interplay between gas molecular properties and characteristic pore dimensions [9]. This approach enables the upscaling of microstructural features to macroscale oxygen flux profiles, critical for predicting TGO growth kinetics and stress evolution at the ceramic-metal interface.

Viscous diffusion, governed by pressure-driven bulk flow, exhibits negligible dependence on molecular properties and is primarily influenced by macroscopic pressure gradients within the porous medium. In contrast, molecular diffusion and Knudsen diffusion regimes are delineated by the Knudsen number [ K_n ]. The Knudsen number [ K_n ] is a dimensionless parameter defined as the ratio of the mean free path [ \lambda ] to a characteristic diameter [ d_p ] of the pores [9]. The characteristic pore diameter [ d_p ] is evaluated based on the average pore size in porous media. The molecular mean free path [ \lambda ] is expressed as

[ \lambda = \frac{k_B T}{\sqrt{2}\rho d_p^2} ]

where [ P ] is the gas pressure, [ d_p ] is the effective diameter of a gas molecule, [ k_B ] is the Boltzmann constant, and [ T ] is the temperature of the gas [9].

Molecular diffusion occurs when the [ K_n ] is less than [ 0.1 ], a regime in which gas molecules traverse the porous matrix via Brownian motion, characterized by frequent intermolecular collisions that dominate the transport process. Conversely, when [ K_n ] exceeds [ 10 ], Knudsen diffusion becomes the primary transport mechanism, wherein gas molecules interact predominantly with the pore walls rather than each other, leading to a collision frequency governed by the surface-to-volume ratio of the porous medium. In the transitional regime where [ K_n ] ranges from [ 0.1 ] to [ 10 ], both molecular and Knudsen diffusion mechanisms contribute significantly to the overall gas transport, necessitating a coupled treatment of intermolecular and wall collisions. Furthermore, the intrinsic tortuosity of the porous TC structure, arising from its complex interconnected pore network, introduces an additional layer of complexity to gas transport dynamics. This tortuosity factor effectively lengthens the mean diffusion path [ L ] for gas molecules, thereby reducing the apparent diffusion coefficient and impeding mass transport through the medium.

In the context of gas turbine environments, the combustion gas mixture—comprising approximately [ 13\% ] oxygen [ (\mathrm{O}_2) ] and [ 74\% ] nitrogen [ (\mathrm{N}_2) ] by volume [33]—exhibits distinct transport behaviors dictated by their respective molecular properties (see Table 1 for physical parameters). Using the kinetic theory of gases, the Knudsen numbers for these species within the porous TC are calculated to fall within the range of [ 0.1 ]–[ 10 ] across typical operating conditions, indicating that molecular and Knudsen diffusion mechanisms contribute synergistically to the overall oxygen transport dynamics in the porous TC.

In the gas diffusion process, Knudsen diffusion and molecular diffusion usually compete with each other via a "resistance in series" approach [9]. For oxygen diffusion in the porous TC layer, the effective diffusion coefficient [ D_{\mathrm{eff}} ] can be expressed as [35]:

[ D^{\mathrm{eff}} = \phi^{2}\left(\frac{1}{D_{\mathrm{m}}} + \frac{1}{D_{\mathrm{K}}}\right)^{-1} \qquad (1) ]

where [ \phi ] is the porosity of the microstructure in porous TC, respectively. [ D_{\mathrm{m}} ] and [ D_{\mathrm{K}} ] are the molecular diffusivity and Knudsen diffusivity of gas, respectively [9],

[ D_{\mathrm{m}} = \frac{0.00186T^{3/2}}{P\theta^{2}\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}\left(\frac{1}{M_{\mathrm{air}}} + \frac{1}{M}\right)^{1/2} \qquad (2) ]

[ D_{\mathrm{K}} = \frac{d_{\mathrm{p}}}{3}\sqrt{\frac{8RT}{\pi M}} \qquad (3) ]

where [ M_{\mathrm{air}} ] is the molecular mass per unit mole of the main gas within the TC, [ M ] is the molecular mass per unit mole of the diffusing gas, [ \theta ] is the average collision diameter of the main gas and the diffusing gas, [ \Omega_{\mathrm{m}} ] is the dimensionless collision integral of the main gas and the diffusing gas, [ P ] is the gas pressure, [ R ] is gas constant, and [ T ] is temperature. For the oxygen diffusion in porous TC, the main gas is nitrogen, and the diffusing gas is oxygen. According to the physical parameters in Table 1, the molecular diffusivity and Knudsen diffusivity coefficients of oxygen are [ 1.8\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m}^2/\mathrm{s} ] and [ 3.18\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m}^2/\mathrm{s} ], respectively.

For the oxygen diffusion in the TC layer, the diffusion flux [ J ] in the TC layer is directly proportional to the concentration gradient [ \nabla c ] and can be described as [ J = -D^{\mathrm{eff}}\nabla c ]. The distribution of oxygen concentration is governed by the corresponding conservation [36]:

[ \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot J = 0 \qquad (4) ]

Besides, considering the absorption effect that the oxygen ions are trapped in the regions around the pores [37,38], Eq. (4) can be modified as [39]:

[ \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot J = -\alpha c \qquad (5) ]

Based on the Langmuir isotherm [40], the fraction of the total available adsorption is described as [ \alpha = \frac{kP}{kP+1} ], and [ k ] is the adsorption equilibrium constant. At high pressures, the fraction [ \alpha ] increases asymptotically to [ 1 ].

The diffusion of nitrogen in the BC and TGO layers is ignored in this work as the nitrogen does not participate in the reactions forming the TGO. The oxygen diffusion in BC and TGO can be modelled based on the theory for the diffusion of solute atoms in the solid. In this context, the diffusion flux [ \mathbf{J} ] in the BC and TGO layers can be described as [19,31]:

[ \mathbf{J} = -\frac{Dc}{RT}\nabla\mu \qquad (6) ]

where [ \mu ] is the chemical potential of the gases in solids, and [ D ] is the diffusion coefficient of oxygen in the BC layer or TGO layer.

Based on thermodynamics, the hydrostatic stress alters the local chemical potential, and thus the chemical potential [ \mu ] with stress effect can be expressed as [26]:

[ \mu = \mu_0 + RT\ln c - \sigma_m\Omega \qquad (7) ]

where [ \mu_0 ] is the reference chemical potential, [ \Omega ] is partial molar volume of gases, and [ \sigma_m ] is the hydrostatic stress.

The diffusion coefficients of the gas in BC and TGO can be expressed as [41]:

[ D = D_0\exp\left(-\frac{Q}{RT}\right) \qquad (8) ]

where [ D_0 ] and [ Q ] are constants. According to the literature [31], the diffusion coefficients of oxygen in the BC and TGO at [ 1373.15\mathrm{K} ] are [ 2.82\times10^{-12}\mathrm{m}^2/\mathrm{s} ] and [ 2.63\times10^{-13}\mathrm{m}^2/\mathrm{s} ], respectively. For the oxygen diffusion in BC and TGO, the distribution of oxygen concentration is governed by the mass conservation equation [13]:

[ \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = -S \qquad (9) ]

where [ S ] is a sink term representing the amount of oxygen consumed during the formation of TGO.

#### 2.1.2. The growth of TGO

The formation mechanism of TGO layer originates from oxidation reactions occurring at the TC/BC interface, where oxygen permeating through TC layer interacts with aluminum ions migrating from BC layer. This oxidation process progressively transforms the BC layer into TGO through solid-state diffusion. While both oxygen and aluminum species participate in this interfacial reaction, extensive studies have established oxygen diffusion through the TC layer as the rate-controlling mechanism. This predominance stems from the significantly higher diffusivity of oxygen in yttria-stabilized zirconia TC layers compared to aluminum diffusivity in the developing TGO structure [27]. Consequently, our computational model justifiably neglects aluminum diffusion contributions for simplification.

To mathematically characterize TGO growth kinetics, we employ a phase-field approach tracking the temporal evolution of TGO volume fraction [ n ] within the reaction zone. The parameter [ n ] serves as a transformation metric, where [ n=0 ] corresponds to pristine BC material and [ n=1 ] represents complete conversion to TGO. The temporal progression of oxidation follows a reaction-rate equation expressed as [23]:

[ \frac{\partial n}{\partial t} = \xi(1-n)c \qquad (10) ]

where [ \xi ] is the reaction constant of proportionality that can be calibrated by the experimentally measured oxide thickness. This formulation inherently accounts for the self-limiting nature of TGO growth, as the [ (1-n) ] term introduces decreasing reaction probability with increasing TGO thickness. Here, the value of [ \xi ] is determined as [ 1\times10^{-4} ] [13].

The rate of TGO formation can be related to the sink term [ S ] as follows [6]:

[ S = \kappa\frac{\partial n}{\partial t} \qquad (11) ]

where [ \kappa ] is the concentration of oxygen consumed to form per mole of TGO and expressed as [ \kappa = \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3}}{M_{\mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3}}\times\eta_{\mathrm{O}_2} ] [31]. Here, [ \eta_{\mathrm{O}_2} ] is the stoichiometric ratio of oxygen, [ \rho_{\mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3} ] and [ M_{\mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3} ] are the density and molar mass of [ \mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3 ].

#### 2.1.3. Stress and deformation in TBCs

The quasi-static treatment of mechanical equilibrium is physically justified by the significant timescale disparity between stress relaxation processes (characteristic time [ \approx10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s} ]) and oxygen-dominated transport phenomena (characteristic time [ \approx10^{-6}\ \mathrm{s} ]). This temporal decoupling permits the mechanical analysis to employ a time-independent formulation. We also assume that the deformation of TBCs during the oxidation falls within the small deformation framework, and thus the strain can be expressed as:

[ \epsilon = \frac{1}{2}\left((\nabla u)^T + \nabla u\right) \qquad (12) ]

During the oxidation, the total strain of the TGO layer can be expressed as:

[ \epsilon = \epsilon_{\mathrm{el}} + \epsilon_{\mathrm{g}} \qquad (13) ]

where [ \epsilon_{\mathrm{el}} ] is the elastic strain, and [ \epsilon_{\mathrm{g}} ] is the growth strain of TGO. The formation of the TGO is typically associated with the volumetric expansion of the material through a growth strain tensor [23]:

[ \epsilon_{\mathrm{g}} = n\gamma\mathrm{I} \qquad (14) ]

where [ \gamma ] is the value of growth strain associated to the newly formed TGO, and [ \mathrm{I} ] is a second-order identity tensor. In view of this strain decomposition, the growth strain provides an important coupling between the oxidation process and the associated state of stress.

Assuming that these layers are isotropic linear elastic materials, the constitutive laws for each layer can be expressed as follows:

[ \sigma = \mathbf{K}:\epsilon_{\mathrm{el}} \qquad (15) ]

where [ \sigma ] is the stress components, [ \mathbf{K} ] is the stiffness matrix. For the BC-TGO mixture zone, a rule of mixtures is adopted to determine its stiffness and other parameters, as expressed by

[ \mathbf{K} = (1-n)\mathbf{K}_{\mathrm{BC}} + n\mathbf{K}_{\mathrm{TGO}} \qquad (16) ]

The stress in the TBCs has to satisfy the mechanical equilibrium condition,

[ \nabla\cdot\sigma = 0 \qquad (17) ]

### 2.2. Dynamic model of oxidation-induced failure in TBCs

#### 2.2.1. Phase field description of damage

The computational modelling of crack initiation and propagation in TBCs presents significant challenges due to the inherent geometric complexities of sharp crack discontinuities and the dynamic evolution of TGO growth induced stress fields. These difficulties can be effectively addressed through diffusive crack modelling based on the phase field methodology. The fundamental principle of this approach lies in replacing sharp crack discontinuities with a regularized representation through the introduction of a continuous scalar field that quantifies material degradation states. Therefore, a scalar field called phase field is introduced to represent the crack. Let [ \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\ (d\in\{1,2,3\}) ] be an open domain describing a cracked solid, with [ D ] being the space dimension and [ \partial\Omega ] its boundary. [ \Gamma ] represents the cracks in the multi-dimensional space. In a regularized framework, the crack geometry is approximated by a smeared representation defined by a scalar parameter [ c ]. We take a one-dimensional infinitely expanded bar subjected to tension at both ends as an example, and the Dirac delta function is usually used to describe the cracking state of the material. In the classical crack description method, as shown in Fig. 2a and c, the sharp crack description function belongs to a discontinuous function with characterizing for [ c=0 ] the undamaged state and for [ c=1 ] the fully-cracked state of the material. In numerical solution, it is difficult to describe the crack state of the entire domain with a continuous function, which often causes problems such as convergence difficulties. In contrast, the diffusive crack, as shown in Fig. 2b and d, describes the crack behavior of the material through the phase field variable. The defined scalar field is related to the continuum theory of damage, where the scalar damage field describes in a homogenized macroscopic sense the development of cracks. Therefore, when the phase-field method simulates the cracking process, it can avoid the singular stress fields near the crack tip.

**Fig. 2.** Discrete and diffusive cracks: (a) Traditional discrete crack; (b) diffusive crack with finite width; (c) Schematic diagram of phase field representation for discrete cracks; (d) Schematic diagram of phase field representation for diffusive cracks.

With this viewpoint in mind, we approximate the diffusive crack by the exponential function [42]:

[ c(x) = e^{-\frac{|x|}{l_0}} \qquad (18) ]

where [ l_0 ] is the regularization parameter of the crack, which controls the characteristic width of the crack. When the [ l_0 ] approaches [ 0 ], the diffusive crack can degenerate into a discrete crack.

Under the Dirichlet-type boundary conditions of [ (c(0)=1,\ c(\pm\infty)=0) ], Eq. (18) is the solution of the equation as follows:

[ c(x) - l_0^2 c''(x) = 0 \qquad (19) ]

Suppose there is a differentiable function defined on the [ \Omega ] region that satisfies:

[ \delta\mathbf{I} = (c - l^2 c'')\delta c \qquad (20) ]

Integrating Eq. (20) over the [ \Omega ] region, the expression of the differentiable function can be obtained:

[ I(c) = \int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\left(c^2 + l^2|\nabla c|^2\right)d\Omega \qquad (21) ]

This differential equation Eq. (21) is the Euler equation of the variational principle:

[ c = \mathrm{Arg}\left\{\inf_{c\in W}I(c)\right\} \qquad (22) ]

with

[ W = \{c\in\mathbb{R}\mid c(0)=1,\ c(\pm\infty)=0\} ]

According to the construction conditions of the model, there is the following equation:

[ d\Omega = \Gamma dx \qquad (23) ]

Substituting Eq. (23) into Eq. (21), we can obtain [ I(c)=\Gamma\cdot l_0 ], which thereby establishes the correlation between the function [ I ] and the crack surface [ \Gamma ]. The regularized crack surface [ \Gamma ] can be expressed as:

[ \Gamma = \frac{1}{2l_0}\int_{\Omega}\left(c^2 + l_0^2|\nabla c|^2\right)d\Omega \qquad (24) ]

[ \gamma ] denotes the crack density function per unit volume, and can be defined by

[ \gamma(c,\nabla c) = \frac{1}{2l_0}\left(c^2(x) + l_0^2|\nabla c|^2\right) \qquad (25) ]

We extend the one-dimensional phase field fracture situation to the multi-dimensional space [ \Omega ] as shown in Fig. 3. The body [ \Omega ] has an external boundary [ \partial\Omega ] and internal discontinuity boundary [ \Gamma ]. The displacement of body [ \Omega ] at time [ t ] is denoted by [ \mathbf{u}(x,t)\subset\mathbb{R}^d ], where [ \mathbf{x} ] is the position vector. [ \overline{\mathbf{u}} ] represents the displacement boundary condition, [ \overline{\mathbf{t}} ] represents the force boundary condition. The displacement field satisfies the time-dependent Dirichlet boundary conditions on [ \partial\Omega_u ], and also the time-dependent Neumann conditions on [ \partial\Omega_t ]. We also consider a body force [ \mathbf{f} ] acted on the body [ \Omega ] and a traction [ \mathbf{b} ] on the boundary.

**Fig. 3.** Discrete and diffusive cracks in multi-dimensional space: (a) discrete crack; (b) diffusive crack with finite width.

Similar to the one-dimensional phase field fracture model, the governing equation of the phase field variable in the multi-dimensional space can be expressed as:

[ \begin{array}{rl} c(\mathbf{x}) - l_0^2\Delta c(\mathbf{x}) = 0 & \mathrm{in}\ \Omega\\ c(\mathbf{x}) = 1 & \mathrm{on}\ \Gamma\\ \nabla c(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{n} = 1 & \mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega \end{array} \qquad (26) ]

where [ \Delta ] is the Laplace operator, [ \nabla ] is the gradient operator, [ \mathbf{x} ] represents the spatial position of a certain point in the material, and [ \mathbf{n} ] represents the outward normal direction of the boundary. The crack surface density [ \gamma ] in the multi-dimensional space can be expressed as:

[ \gamma(c,\nabla c) = \frac{1}{2l_0}\left(c^2(x) + l_0^2|\nabla c|^2\right) \qquad (27) ]

In isotropic materials, the critical energy release rate [ G_c ] is usually a fixed value and does not affect the crack size and crack path. Therefore, the surface energy [ \psi_c ] of the crack can be obtained by integrating over the crack surface density [ \gamma ].

[ \psi_c = \int_{\Omega}G_c\gamma(c,\nabla c)\mathrm{d}\Omega \qquad (28) ]

#### 2.2.2. Theory of brittle fracture

From the perspective of energy, the Griffith theory states that when the energy release rate at the crack tip exceeds the energy required to form the crack area, the crack propagates, which can explain the crack propagation behavior in brittle materials. However, the Griffith theory is difficult to describe the initiation behavior before crack propagation. To overcome the deficiency of this theory, a variational approach for fracture problems according to Griffith's theory has been proposed [43]. It states that the required energy to create a fracture surface per unit area is equal to the critical fracture energy density [ G_c ], which is also commonly referred to as the critical energy release rate.

The total potential energy [ U(\mathbf{u},\Gamma) ] can be expressed in terms of the elastic energy [ \psi_e ], fracture energy [ \psi_c ] and energy due to external forces:

[ U(\mathbf{u},\Gamma) = \int_{\Omega}\psi_e(\epsilon(\mathbf{u}))\mathrm{d}\Omega + \int_{\Gamma}G_c\mathrm{d}\Gamma - \int_{\Omega}\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{u}\mathrm{d}\Omega - \int_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{t}\cdot\mathbf{u}\mathrm{d}S \qquad (29) ]

Under the given boundary conditions and the irreversible condition of fracture, the governing equation of the crack surface can be obtained by finding the extremum of Eq. (29). However, the solution of the discrete crack model is still a complex problem. To transform the discrete crack field variables into globally continuous variables, the regularized phase field is introduced into the functional form of the Griffith theory [43]:

[\Pi(\mathbf{u},c)=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}g(c)\epsilon(\mathbf{u}):\mathbb{C}:\epsilon(\mathbf{u})\,\mathrm{d}\Omega+\int_{\Omega}\frac{G_c}{2l_0}\left(c^2+l_0^2|\nabla c|^2\right)\mathrm{d}\Omega\qquad(30)]

where [ \mathbb{C} ] is elastic tensor, [ g(c) ] is the stiffness degradation function of the material.

This model is based on the principle of energy minimization and could continuously describe complex fracture modes of the bulk material, from the crack-free state to crack initiation and propagation, as well as crack bifurcation and convergence. According to the principle of energy minimization, the system will adjust the internal crack area to minimize its total energy. The energy that drives the evolution of the phase-field is mainly affected by the strain energy and the degradation function.

To ensure that the crack is driven by tensile load, it is essential to decompose the elastic energy into tensile and compressive parts. The spherical-deviatoric decomposition method proposed by Amor et al. [44] can solve this problem. This decomposition method is based on the orthogonal decomposition of the linearized strain tensor, dividing the strain tensor into volumetric strain and deviatoric strain, and further dividing the total strain energy density [ \psi_e ] into the sum of the spherical part [ \psi_e^{\mathrm{vol}} ] and the deviatoric part [ \psi_e^{\mathrm{dev}} ], which can be expressed respectively as:

[ \psi_e^{\mathrm{vol}} = \frac{1}{2}K_n\theta:\theta \qquad (31) ]

[ \psi_e^{\mathrm{dev}} = \mu\epsilon^{\mathrm{dev}}:\epsilon^{\mathrm{dev}} \qquad (32) ]

where [ K_n ] is bulk modulus, [ \theta ] is volumetric strain and [ \epsilon^{\mathrm{dev}} ] is deviatoric strain.

When the volumetric strain is greater than [ 0 ], the material will be damaged and further lead to the occurrence of fracture damage. Here, the decomposition approach in Ref. [22] is adopted to guarantee that the evolution of the phase-field will only be induced by the tensile part of the elastic energy density, while compressive stress will not contribute to the propagation of the crack. Therefore, the [ \psi_e^{+} ] and [ \psi_e^{-} ] can be defined as:

[ \psi_e^{+} = \psi_e^{\mathrm{vol}}(\theta)_{+} + \mu\epsilon^{\mathrm{dev}}:\epsilon^{\mathrm{dev}} \qquad (33) ]

[ \psi_e^{-} = \psi_e^{\mathrm{vol}}(\theta)_{-} \qquad (34) ]

where operators [ \langle x\rangle_{+} ] and [ \langle x\rangle_{-} ] are defined as [ \langle x\rangle_{+} = \frac{|x|+x}{2} ] and [ \langle x\rangle_{-} = \frac{|x|-x}{2} ].

The monotonically decreasing function [ g(c) ] describes the degradation of stored energy due to evolving damage by the phase field:

[ g(0)=1,\quad g(1)=0,\quad g'(1)=0 \qquad (35) ]

The first two conditions include the limits for the unbroken and the fully broken case. The last constraint ensures that the elastic driving force converges to a final value if the phase field converges to the fully broken state [ c=1 ]. A simple function that has the above properties is:

[ g(c) = (1-c)^n + k,\quad n\geq2 \qquad (36) ]

where a small positive parameter [ k\sim0 ] is introduced to Eq. (36) to circumvent the full degradation of the elastic energy density at a fully broken state [ c=1 ]. It is chosen as small as possible such that the algebraic conditioning number of the applied numerical discretization method remains well-posed for partly-broken systems.

#### 2.2.3. Effect of fracture phase field on deformation

In this section, this coupling is realized by modifying the elastic stiffness as the evolution of phase field fracture variable.

Since the mechanical equilibrium usually completes much faster than the oxygen diffusion and oxidation reaction, it is reasonable to treat the mechanical equilibrium as a static phenomenon. We also assume that the deformation of TBCs during the oxidation falls within the small deformation framework, and thus the strain can be expressed as:

[ \epsilon = \frac{1}{2}\left((\nabla u)^T + \nabla u\right) \qquad (37) ]

During the oxidation, the total strain of the TGO layer can be expressed as:

[ \epsilon = \epsilon_e + \epsilon_g \qquad (38) ]

where [ \epsilon_e ] is the elastic strain, and [ \epsilon_g ] is the growth strain of TGO. The formation of the TGO is typically associated with the volumetric expansion of the material through a growth strain tensor [ \epsilon_g = \eta\gamma\mathrm{I} ] [23], where [ \gamma ] is the value of growth strain associated to the newly formed TGO, and [ \mathrm{I} ] is a second-order identity tensor. In view of this strain decomposition, the growth strain provides an important coupling between the oxidation process and the associated state of stress.

The total strain of TC, BC, and SUB layers can be expressed as:

[ \epsilon = \epsilon_e \qquad (39) ]

The component of Cauchy stress tensor given by:

[ \sigma = g(c)\frac{\partial\psi_e}{\partial\epsilon} = g(c)\left(\frac{\partial\psi_e^{+}(\epsilon)}{\partial\epsilon} + \frac{\partial\psi_e^{-}(\epsilon)}{\partial\epsilon}\right) \qquad (40) ]

According to the linear elastic constitutive model, the stiffness tensor [ \mathbb{C}_c ] reduced by phase-field is given by:

[ \mathbb{C}_c = g(c)\mathbb{C} \qquad (41) ]

#### 2.2.4. Governing equations

According to Griffith's fracture theory, the energy formulation for the cracked elastic system can be systematically derived as follows.

The kinetic energy of the cracked elastic body is expressed as:

[ T = \int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\rho\dot{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{u}}\,d\Omega \qquad (42) ]

The total potential energy [ U(\mathbf{u},\Gamma) ] combines elastic and surface energies:

[ U(\mathbf{u},\Gamma) = \int_{\Omega}\psi_e(\epsilon(\mathbf{u}))d\Omega + \int_{\Gamma}G_c d\Gamma - \int_{\Omega}\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{u}d\Omega - \int_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{t}\cdot\mathbf{u}dS \qquad (43) ]

The Lagrangian energy functional [ \Pi(\mathbf{u},\Gamma)=T-U ] of the cracked system can be expressed as:

[ \Pi(\mathbf{u},\Gamma) = \int_{\Omega}\rho\dot{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{u}}d\Omega - \int_{\Omega}\psi_e(\epsilon(\mathbf{u}))d\Omega - \int_{\Gamma}G_c d\Gamma + \int_{\Omega}\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{u}d\Omega + \int_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{t}\cdot\mathbf{u}dS \qquad (44) ]

Taking the first variation [ \delta\Pi=0 ] with respect to admissible variations [ \delta\epsilon ], [ \delta\mathbf{u} ] and [ \delta c ], we obtain the weak form:

[ \begin{array}{rl} &-\int_{\Omega}\rho\ddot{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\delta\mathbf{u}d\Omega + \int_{\Omega}\sigma:\delta\epsilon d\Omega - \int_{\Omega}\mathbf{f}\cdot\delta\mathbf{u}d\Omega - \int_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{t}\cdot\delta\mathbf{u}dS = 0\\ &\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{G_c}{l_0}c + \frac{\delta g(c)}{\delta c}\psi_e\right)\delta c\,d\Omega + \int_{\Omega}G_c l_0\nabla c\cdot\delta\nabla c\,d\Omega = 0 \end{array} \qquad (45) ]

Applying divergence theorem yields the strong form:

[ \begin{array}{rl} &\nabla\cdot\sigma + \mathbf{f} = \rho\ddot{\mathbf{u}}\\ &\left[\frac{2l_0\psi_e}{G_c} + 1\right]c - l_0^2\Delta c = \frac{2l_0\psi_e}{G_c}\\ &\sigma\cdot\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{t}\quad \mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega_t\\ &\nabla c\cdot\mathbf{n} = 0\quad \mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega\\ &\mathbf{u} = \overline{\mathbf{u}}\quad \mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega_u \end{array} \qquad (46) ]

According to the laws of thermodynamics, crack initiation and propagation represent an irreversible process, whereas the phase field governing equations are reversible. Thus, when cyclic loading is applied to materials, the release of strain energy can lead to crack healing and subsequent re-propagation. To ensure the validity of computational results from phase field fracture models, it is necessary to account for the irreversibility conditions of crack propagation and the boundedness of damage variables. The crack variables should satisfy:

[ \dot{c}\geq0,\quad c\in[0,1] \qquad (47) ]

To enforce the irreversibility condition of crack propagation, a historical variable [ H(\mathbf{x},t) ] is introduced to store the maximum strain energy density experienced during the material deformation process, where [ \mathbf{x} ] denotes the spatial coordinate and [ t ] represents time. Additionally, when accounting for the distinction between local tensile and compressive stresses, based on the energy decomposition method, the historical variable [ H(\mathbf{x},t) ] can be expressed as:

[ H(\mathbf{x},t) = \max_{t\in[0,T]}\psi_e^{+}(\mathbf{x},t) \qquad (48) ]

The stress field and phase-field equations become:

[ \begin{array}{rl} &\nabla\cdot\sigma + \mathbf{f} = \rho\ddot{\mathbf{u}}\\ &\left[\frac{2l_0H}{G_c} + 1\right]c - l_0^2\Delta c = \frac{2l_0H}{G_c} \end{array} \qquad (49) ]

### 2.3. Numerical implementation

#### 2.3.1. Overall algorithm

The phase field's governing equations consist of two highly coupled nonlinear equations, which pose a challenge in solving the system obtained from their finite element discretization. Currently, two main approaches are available for solving such nonlinear coupled equations: the monolithic solver method and the alternating minimization method. The alternating minimization method involves sequentially solving each of the governing equations independently, which helps to decouple the system and simplify the computational process. This approach is particularly effective in improving convergence for highly coupled nonlinear systems. Therefore, we employ the alternating minimization method to sequentially solve the phase field, displacement field, and historical variables. The relationships among the modules used in the solution process are illustrated in Fig. 4.

**Fig. 4.** Relationships among the modules used in the solution process.

Initially, the solid mechanics module computes the stress and strain fields of the material. Subsequently, the "storage module" retains the outcomes of the strain field, such as the magnitude and direction of the volumetric strain and the elastic energy. Next, the elastic energy corresponding to the volumetric strain is calculated and fed into the "historical strain module" to solve for and update the maximum strain energy density experienced by the material. The updated strain energy density is then utilized to solve for the phase field. Finally, in the solid mechanics module, the updated phase field solution and the previously stored strain results are employed to modify the stiffness degradation behavior, thereby updating the strain field solution and proceeding to the next time step.

In the implementation, COMSOL segregated solver is utilized to address the phase field fracture problem, with residuals for each governing equation computed using the standard Newton-Raphson method. To enhance computational stability, we adopt an implicit generalized-[ \alpha ] time-stepping scheme. When solving for the phase field variables at time [ t ], predictions for the phase field, displacement field variables, and historical variables are first made based on the results from previous time steps. Specifically, linear extrapolation from the previous solution is used to construct the initial guess for the nonlinear system to be solved at the current time step. Through the iterative scheme described above, solutions across the entire time domain are obtained.

While real APS TBC interfaces are more irregular, such as the multi-scale asperities, non-uniform wavelength, and local sharp protrusions, the sinusoidal model captures the essential topological feature of rough interfaces (periodic peaks and valleys) that dominates oxidation-induced stress and crack behavior.

### 3.2. Isothermal oxidation experiments

Prior to conducting the isothermal oxidation test, all samples undergo a high-vacuum heat treatment at a vacuum level of [ 1\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Pa} ] and a temperature of [ 1100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} ] for [ 4\,\mathrm{h} ]. This process results in the formation of a uniform alumina TGO layer with an approximate thickness of [ 1\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] at the TC/BC interface, which is a continuous, dense, and thin [ \alpha\mathrm{-Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3 ] oxide scale serving as a diffusion barrier with low oxygen diffusivity. The growth behavior of the TGO layer is subsequently evaluated through isothermal oxidation experiments conducted in a high-temperature furnace under static air at atmospheric pressure. Three groups of samples are positioned in alumina crucibles in the furnace at room temperature ([ \sim25^{\circ}\mathrm{C} ]) and then gradually heated to [ 1100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} ] with a heating rate of [ 5^{\circ}\mathrm{C/min} ]. After the specified oxidation time, the oxidized samples are cooled down to room temperature with a cooling rate of [ 5^{\circ}\mathrm{C/min} ].

The growth of TGO can be evaluated by measuring the mean TGO thickness in relation to the oxidation time and TC porosity. The oxidized samples are embedded with resin and cut out to minimize damage. The TGO morphology and thickness of the three groups of TBC samples are characterized using SEM.

## 4. Results and discussions

### 4.1. Oxidation evolution in TBCs

Fig. 8 presents the microstructural characteristics of the TGO in TBCs with a [ 7\% ] TC porosity, following exposure to different isothermal durations at [ 1373.15\,\mathrm{K} ]. TGO initiates formation at the TC/BC interface. It typically has a two-layered structure: the black layer adjacent to the BC is alumina, and the grey layer near the TC is mixed oxide. Due to vacuum heat treatment, the initially formed TGO is mainly composed of alumina (i.e., the black region). This alumina forms a uniform thin layer at the TC/BC interface. Additionally, TGO thickness increases with prolonged oxidation, which is consistent with our previous findings.

Then, we investigate how TC porosity influences TGO thickness under fixed isothermal oxidation ([ 200\,\mathrm{h} ] at [ 1373.15\,\mathrm{K} ]), as shown in Fig. 9. Mean TGO thickness is quantified using the open-source software ImageJ. Average thickness is calculated by normalizing the TGO area to the corresponding path length. This parameter is then used to evaluate the relationship between TGO growth rate and TC porosity. At [ 2\% ] TC porosity, the mean TGO thickness is approximately [ 4\,\mu\mathrm{m} ]. When porosity increases to [ 7\% ], the average thickness rises to [ 5\,\mu\mathrm{m} ]. A further increase in porosity to [ 15\% ] leads to a slight rise in mean thickness, reaching [ 5.5\,\mu\mathrm{m} ]. These results show a positive correlation between TC porosity and TGO thickness. The magnitude of thickness increment decreases as porosity rises. Besides oxidation time and porosity, interface roughness also modulates TGO growth (Fig. 10). As interface roughness increases, TGO becomes thicker at peak positions and thinner at valley positions.

**Fig. 11.** Validation of TGO growth with the change of porosity and oxidation time.

To validate the model's ability to capture the core factors (oxidation time and TC porosity) governing TGO growth, we compare experimental measurements with numerical predictions directly. This comparison is conducted at an interface amplitude of [ 10\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] (Fig. 11). The results reveal a clear trend: TGO thickness increases significantly with longer oxidation time, but the growth rate gradually diminishes. Notably, higher TC porosity correlates with a marked acceleration in TGO growth kinetics. Strong quantitative agreement between experimental and simulated data confirms that the diffusion-oxidation model effectively captures the spatiotemporal evolution of TGO thickness. A minor discrepancy is observed: numerically predicted TGO thickness is slightly higher than experimental measurements. This deviation is likely due to dispersed closed pores inherent to the TC microstructure. These pores restrict oxygen transport pathways, thereby reducing the oxidation rate compared to the model's idealized open-porosity assumptions.

To extend the model's validation context and refine the TGO growth mechanism, we further explore the coupled effects of interface amplitude and porosity on TGO oxidation kinetics at peaks and valleys (Fig. 12). As interface roughness increases, TGO thickness at peaks gradually increases (Fig. 12a, c, 12e). Moreover, higher roughness enhances the influence of porosity on TGO growth rate at peaks. In contrast, increased interface roughness leads to a gradual decrease in TGO thickness at valleys (Fig. 12b, d, 12f). Compared to TGO oxidation at peaks, interface roughness weakens the impact of porosity on TGO oxidation at valleys. This trend matches the observations in Fig. 10. Although the model validation in Fig. 11 focuses on oxidation time and porosity, the coupled effect of roughness and porosity (observed in Fig. 12) aligns with the model's underlying diffusion-stress mechanism. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the numerical model effectively captures the key factors governing TGO growth in experiments.

### 4.2. Oxidation induced stress in TBCs

Fig. 13a illustrates the TGO morphology of TBCs specimens with [ 7\% ] porosity and varying interfacial roughness after [ 1000\,\mathrm{h} ] of oxidation, with red regions denoting the oxidized TGO layer. As the interfacial amplitude increases, the non-uniformity of the TGO thickness becomes more pronounced. At an interfacial amplitude of [ 5\,\mu\mathrm{m} ], the TGO thickness at the peak is approximately [ 9\,\mu\mathrm{m} ], while that at the valley is around [ 7.5\,\mu\mathrm{m} ], indicating a largely uniform thickness distribution along the interface. When the amplitude increases to [ 10\,\mu\mathrm{m} ], the TGO thickness at the peak rises to [ \sim11\,\mu\mathrm{m} ], whereas the valley thickness decreases to [ \sim7\,\mu\mathrm{m} ], with a discernible thickness gradient between peaks and valleys. As the amplitude further increases to [ 15\,\mu\mathrm{m} ], the TGO thickness at the peak reaches [ \sim12\,\mu\mathrm{m} ], while the valley thickness drops to [ \sim6.7\,\mu\mathrm{m} ], resulting in a significant discrepancy in thickness between the two regions and a markedly enhanced non-uniformity in TGO distribution across the interface.

To elucidate the cause of the TGO thickness disparity between peaks and valleys, Fig. 13b shows the oxygen concentration distribution within the TGO and BC layers after [ 1000\,\mathrm{h} ] of oxidation. The oxygen diffusion rate at the peak is higher than that at the valley, and the degree of non-uniform oxygen concentration distribution increases with the interfacial roughness. This phenomenon is attributed to the influence of stress on oxygen diffusion, as shown in Fig. 13c. Tensile stress, which promotes oxygen diffusion, is concentrated at the peak of the TGO/BC interface, while compressive stress, which inhibits diffusion, accumulates at the valley. This coupling effect between stress and oxygen diffusion leads to non-uniform TGO growth.

Increased interfacial roughness strengthens the influence of porosity on TGO growth rate at peaks. At high roughness, the TGO thickness difference between peaks with varying porosities becomes more prominent, as the larger tensile stress at high-roughness peaks further facilitates oxygen diffusion, thereby amplifying porosity's effect on oxygen diffusion behavior. In contrast, interfacial roughness weakens porosity's impact on TGO oxidation behavior at valleys. At high roughness, the TGO thickness difference between valleys with different porosities diminishes, because the larger compressive stress at high-roughness valleys further restricts oxygen diffusion, reducing the effect.

### 4.3. The oxidation-induced cracking analysis

Fig. 14a illustrates the influence of TC layer porosity on the damage accumulation behavior at the crack initiation points of the TC/TGO interface when the interface amplitude is [ 5\,\mu\mathrm{m} ]. When the phase field variable is [ 0 ], the coating is in an undamaged state. As oxidation progresses, damage at the TC/TGO interface accumulates slowly, accompanied by an increase in the phase field variable. When the phase field variable exceeds [ 0.4 ], the damage accumulation rate accelerates, leading to crack initiation within a shorter timeframe. Crack initiation at the TC/TGO interface valleys occurs when the phase field variable reaches [ 1 ]. Reducing porosity to less than [ 4\% ] not only retards TGO growth but also delays crack initiation. At a phase field variable of [ 0.4 ], the TGO thickness at the valleys is consistently [ 5\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] across all porosity levels. Fig. 14b depicts the crack initiation and propagation process at the TC/TGO interface for a [ 7\% ] porosity specimen with a [ 5\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] interface amplitude. As oxidation proceeds, cracks first initiate at the valleys, accompanied by stress degradation in the cracked region and stress concentration at the crack tips. With further TGO thickening, cracks propagate laterally along the interface.

When the interface amplitude increases to [ 10\,\mu\mathrm{m} ], the damage accumulation and crack evolution behavior retain core trends while showing slight differences, as illustrated in Fig. 15. Lower porosity delays both TGO growth and crack initiation, yet the critical TGO thickness for crack initiation ([ 5\,\mu\mathrm{m} ]) remains consistent across porosity levels. Compared to the [ 5\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] amplitude case, damage accumulates more slowly at [ 10\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] despite identical critical TGO thicknesses. This slower accumulation is attributed to higher roughness retarding TGO oxidation at valleys. Cracks initiate at the valleys, consistent with the [ 5\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] amplitude case, accompanied by stress redistribution. Subsequent TGO growth drives lateral crack propagation along the TC/TGO interface.

A further increase in interface amplitude to [ 15\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] leads to more distinct changes in damage and crack behavior, as presented in Fig. 16. Fig. 16a shows the damage accumulation behavior under this amplitude: damage accumulates slowly until the phase field variable exceeds [ 0.4 ], after which crack initiation accelerates. Lower porosity still delays crack initiation, but the critical TGO thickness at the valleys increases to [ 6\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] (up from [ 5\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] in [ 5-10\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] amplitudes). Damage accumulation here is slower than at [ 5-10\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] amplitudes, which stems from two factors: retarded TGO oxidation at valleys and an upward shift of crack initiation sites from the TC/TGO interface into the TC layer. Unlike the [ 5-10\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] cases where cracks initiate at the valleys, cracks here first initiate above the valleys, accompanied by stress redistribution. As TGO thickness increases, cracks propagate downward to the TC/TGO interface and then extend laterally along it.

Collectively, these results reveal consistent trends and key differences in TC/TGO interface behavior with increasing interface amplitude. All amplitudes show that damage accelerates when the phase field variable exceeds [ 0.4 ], lower TC porosity delays crack initiation, and cracks originate near valleys (either at or above the valley region). This threshold phase-field variable threshold of [ 0.4 ] aligns with existing fracture criteria for ceramic coatings in the literature under oxidation-induced stress [13]. The threshold for accelerated damage accumulation exhibits clear material dependence, as it is closely linked to the intrinsic mechanical properties of the TC layer in this study. Regarding generalizability, the threshold of [ 0.4 ] is not universally applicable but can be extended to similar material systems. Besides, as amplitude increases, the critical TGO thickness for crack initiation rises from [ 5\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] ([ 5-10\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] amplitudes) to [ 6\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] ([ 15\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] amplitude), damage accumulation slows progressively (due to retarded valley TGO oxidation and, for [ 15\,\mu\mathrm{m} ], upward-shifted crack initiation), and crack initiation sites move from the TC/TGO interface valleys ([ 5-10\,\mu\mathrm{m} ]) to above the valleys in the TC layer ([ 15\,\mu\mathrm{m} ]).

To verify the numerical results, the oxidation-induced failure behavior at the TC/TGO interface (under varying porosity and interfacial roughness) was investigated by characterizing interfacial crack morphologies near the TGO layer after [ 200\,\mathrm{h} ] of oxidation. Prior to analysis, oxidized specimens were vacuum-impregnated with epoxy resin and sectioned using a diamond wire saw to minimize coating damage. Cross-sections were then polished and imaged via SEM to observe TC/TGO interface crack patterns across three specimen groups.

**Fig. 17.** Interface cracking behavior under different porosities after [ 200\,\mathrm{h} ] of oxidation: (a) [ 2\% ], (b) [ 7\% ], (c) [ 15\% ].

Fig. 17 compares TC/TGO interface crack morphologies (after [ 200\,\mathrm{h} ] of oxidation) for specimens with different TC porosities. At [ 2\% ] porosity, the sparse pore network impedes oxygen diffusion, resulting in an average TGO thickness of [ \sim3.5\,\mu\mathrm{m} ], too thin to induce TC cracking. Increasing porosity to [ 7\% ] enhances oxygen diffusion and TGO growth, leading to an average TGO thickness of [ 4.8\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] and localized cracking at the TC/TGO interface. At [ 15\% ] porosity, the average TGO thickness reaches [ 5.5\,\mu\mathrm{m} ]; cracks initiate and propagate along the interface, eventually causing extensive TC delamination. These results confirm that higher porosity accelerates interfacial oxidation failure during prolonged exposure by facilitating oxygen ingress and TGO thickening.

Further analysis (under [ 200\,\mathrm{h} ] of oxidation) explored the effect of interfacial roughness on TC/TGO interface crack behavior, using specimens with [ 15\% ] and [ 7\% ] porosity (Fig. 18a and b). Two roughness-dependent failure modes were identified: at lower roughness, cracks initiate and propagate directly along the TC/TGO interface; at higher roughness, crack initiation shifts to a region just above the interface, followed by lateral propagation. This transition aligns with the interface oxidation failure mechanisms reported by Echsler et al. [46], which document similar crack patterns (Fig. 18c and d).

**Fig. 18.** Crack patterns near the TC/TGO interface affected by roughness after [ 200\,\mathrm{h} ] of oxidation: (a) interface with a porosity of [ 15\% ]; (b) interface with a porosity of [ 7\% ]; (c) interface with an amplitude of approximately [ 10\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] [46] (oxidation temperature [ 1050^{\circ}\mathrm{C} ], oxidation time [ 300\,\mathrm{h} ]); (d) interface with an amplitude of approximately [ 15\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] [46] (oxidation temperature [ 1100^{\circ}\mathrm{C} ], oxidation time [ 300\,\mathrm{h} ]).

Under the same oxidation duration, low roughness and high porosity leads to interface cracking, attributed to reduced local oxidation temperatures at the interface and thinner TGO. In contrast, high roughness and low porosity promotes cracking above the interface—this requires greater TGO thickness and longer oxidation times, likely due to enhanced stress concentration at interfacial asperities. These experimental observations validate the computationally predicted TC/TGO interface oxidation failure behavior, especially roughness's role in modulating crack initiation sites and growth kinetics. This phenomenon occurs because a high-amplitude ([ >15\,\mu\mathrm{m} ]) rough interface with low porosity creates tortuous oxygen diffusion paths: this reduces valley oxidation rates and alters stress distribution (tensile at peaks, compressive at valleys), causing cracks to deviate from the TC/TGO interface.

The synergistic effect of low TC porosity and high TC/TGO interface amplitude on spallation resistance provides actionable guidance for industrial coating processes, with critical consideration of property tradeoffs. A graded porosity design ([ 2-4\% ] near the interface, [ 6-8\% ] in the outer layer) balances spallation resistance and insulation performance. For the BC layer, a uniform thickness combined with a rough surface ([ 15-20\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] amplitude) avoids uneven TGO growth and stress accumulation at sharp asperities, while minimizing adhesion degradation from high roughness.

## 5. Conclusion

This study establishes a synergistic mechanism by which porosity and interface roughness regulate oxidation failure in TBCs. By integrating an interface oxidation model with phase-field fracture theory, the main conclusions are as follows:

1. TC porosity below [ 4\% ] significantly impedes oxygen transport by reducing the effective oxygen diffusion coefficient, and slows TGO growth at the TC/TGO interface. This not only lowers the interfacial oxidation rate but also reduces the crack driving force by mitigating oxide-induced growth stress. Compared to specimens with porosity above [ 7\% ], porosity below [ 4\% ] delays crack initiation and reduces the crack propagation velocity.

2. Interface amplitude exceeding [ 15\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] induces a more pronounced non-uniformity in TGO thickness. For instance, with [ 15\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] amplitude, the TGO thickness at peaks is approximately [ 80\% ] greater than that at valleys, whereas with [ 5\,\mu\mathrm{m} ] amplitude, this difference is only about [ 20\% ]. Meanwhile, it creates complex stress distributions: tensile stress concentrates at interface peaks to promote oxygen diffusion and TGO growth, while compressive stress accumulates at valleys to inhibit diffusion. This stress-oxidation coupling further exacerbates TGO inhomogeneity and modulates crack initiation sites.

3. The interaction between low porosity (below [ 4\% ]) and high interface amplitude (above [ 15\,\mu\mathrm{m} ]) generates tortuous oxygen diffusion paths due to closed pores in the TC. It also alters the local stress field by enhancing the contrast between peak tensile stress and valley compressive stress. This synergism drives crack initiation to shift from the TC/TGO interface to the TC layer above valleys. Such crack deflection reduces the risk of interfacial delamination, prolonging TC spallation time under typical operating conditions.
