## ABSTRACT

The influence of a thermal cycle fatigue (TCF) loading waveform on cracking behaviors of an atmospheric plasma-sprayed thermal barrier coating (TBC) system was numerically studied using the commercial software. Results showed that the TCF loading waveform considerably affects stress distributions in the TBCs and is the leading factor for the initiation and propagation of cracks in TBCs samples. Under triangular load waveform, the cracks initiate and grow in top-coat (TC) layers. However, under trapezoidal load waveform, the cracks are likely to initiate at an off-valley location and propagate along the TC/thermally-grown-oxide (TGO) interface, causing the complete spallation of the TC layer. Furthermore, the influences of the prior time and holding time on the crack initiation and propagation under the trapezoidal loading conditions were discussed, respectively. Interfacial cracks are likely to initiate when the prior time is prolonged. Under the trapezoidal loading conditions, the location of crack initiation moves from the peak to the off-valley location of a TC/TGO interface as holding time increases. Under a long holding time of the trapezoidal loading conditions, an improved spray quality of TC/TGO interface can increase the service life of TBCs.

## 1. Introduction

For thermal insulation and heat and corrosion resistance, thermal barrier coatings (TBCs) are employed on metallic components under extreme thermal conditions, which can improve the efficiency and service temperature of gas turbines [1]. A typical TBC consists of a ceramic top coat (TC), metallic bond coat (BC), and a thermally grown oxide (TGO) [2]. However, the TGO growth during high temperatures service will cause the thermal mismatch stress and the interfacial cracks initiation at the TC/TGO or TGO/BC interface [3]. These cracks finally lead to the failure and subsequent spallation of TBCs. Thus, it is important to understand the stress distribution and evolution in TBCs caused by TGO growth.

Several studies related to the failure of TBCs have been conducted by experimental methods and finite elements method (FEM) analysis [4-6]. Internal factors, such as the effect of sintering, residual stresses in TC layers, and roughness of interface, and external factors, such as load waveform and erosive actions in a gas environment, can affect the durability of TBCs [7]. Residual stress, which is caused by fast cooling at high temperatures, is one of the most important factors affecting the service lifetime of TBCs [8-10], followed by the presence of TGO between the BC and the TC layer [11,12]. Residual stress, induced by the TGO growth, also causes micro-crack formation in TC/TGO interface [13,14]. The third most crucial factor is thermal load waveform, which causes non-uniform TGO growth and changes the roughness of the interface [15]. A change in load waveform can induce different stress distributions in TBCs, which transform the location of the most dangerous point and causes different failure modes [16-18]. Our previous study showed that the cracking mechanisms of atmospheric-plasma-sprayed (APS) TBCs are dependent on the waveforms of applied thermal cycle fatigue (TCF) loadings. For triangular loading condition, failure close to the TC/TGO interface but within the TC layer is observed, whereas cracks under trapezoidal loading condition preferentially occur at the TC/TGO interface, as shown in Fig. 1.

The FEM is an extremely effective way to calculate the stress distribution of TBCs. Ranjbar-Far et al. [19] evaluated the stress in TBCs considering the effects of inelastic behavior and the morphology of TC/BC interfaces. Jiang et al. [20] and Bialas et al. [21] investigated the cracking behavior of TBCs with different TGO thicknesses. In above studies, the FE models were built with the preset TGO thickness, in which the growth stress due to the TGO growth was ignored. However, the results of stress distribution are different from the analytic methods which are considering the TGO growth stress [22]. In recent years, with the development of FEM, a series of new FE models were established, coupling the effect of TGO growth stress. Shen et al. [23], Li et al. [24] and Gupta et al. [25] calculated the effect of TGO growth on the stress distributions in TBCs using the swelling elements or UMAT subroutines. However, numerical study about the influence of load waveform on stress evolution in TBCs has not been conducted.

The different holding time in load waveforms and TC/TGO interfacial damage are important factors which lead to different growth stresses and induce various failure modes. Therefore, in this work, the influence of TCF loading waveform on cracking behaviors of an APS TBC was numerically studied using commercial software to elucidate the failure mechanism and damage evolution of a TBC under different load waveform and prior oxidation time.

## 2. Numerical modeling method

In this paper, TGO growth can be simulated by the swelling options in ABAQUS/Standard. In addition, the Debond and Fracture Criterion option also was employed in TC/TGO interfaces. To achieve maximum accuracy in the simulation, we utilized two boundary conditions of fixed and equation constraint, which were assigned to the left and right edges of the coating domain, respectively.

### 2.1. Geometric model and mesh

In this paper, a 2D periodic FEM model with single interfacial asperity was established based on the cross-sectional image of the as received TBC samples, as shown in Fig. 2. As a result of the spray process, the TC and BC interfaces were rough. A sine curve was used for the simulation of the waved interface. Based on multipoint measurement and average, the wave length [ L ] of the interface was [ 21.43\ \mu\mathrm{m} ] and amplitude was [ 6.88\ \mu\mathrm{m} ].

The mesh of the finite element model is shown in Fig. 2(c). Four-node bilinear plane strain integration elements are used in TC, TGO, and BC layers. To simulate the separation of the ceramic layer, we adopted cohesive elements (COH2D4). Notably, a swelling subroutine is used to simulate the growth of TGO. Viscous regularization is adopted in FEM analysis, which can improve convergence acceleration.

### 2.3. Cohesive zone model

The traction-separation law used to model the cohesive zones. When interfacial damage [ D ] reaches to 1, the CZM elements are failed and the stiffness drops to 0, which means the cracks initiate and extend along the TGO/TC interface.

Xu et al. [32] indicated that, for interface cohesive zones, the critical stress [ (\sigma_n^0,\sigma_s^0) ] is between 35 and [ 125\ \mathrm{MPa} ], and fracture energy [ \Gamma_n^0 = \Gamma_s^0 ] is between 70 and [ 150\ \mathrm{J}/\mathrm{m}^2 ]. Thus, in the current work, we used the mean of the maximum and minimum of the above material parameters. And the critical opening displacement [ \delta_n^0,\delta_s^0 ] is set to [ 0.6\ \mu\mathrm{m} ].

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. The dominance stress of failure under different load waveforms

The different stress distributions of triangular and trapezoidal load waveforms in the TC layers are first examined. As shown in Figs. 5 and 6, the stress distribution of TBCs under a trapezoidal load is significantly different from that under a triangular load at the end of the cooling process owing to the effect of TGO growth. In this FE model, the initial thickness of the TGO layer is set to [ 0.7\ \mu\mathrm{m} ]. Under the triangular thermal load waveform, the maximum normal tensile stress [ \mathrm{s}_{22} ] is at the off-peak location of the TC/TGO interface. It is worth to mention that under the triangular load waveforms, only the thermal mismatch stress was considered because the thickness of the TGO was constant, and no interfacial damage occurs. However, after holding the temperature for [ 0.68\ \mathrm{h} ], because of TGO layer growth and interfacial damage, the location of maximum [ \mathrm{s}_{22} ] moved from the off-peak location to the off-valley location (plotted in Fig. 6). As the cycle number increases, the [ \mathrm{s}_{22} ] concentration along the sine curve intensifies.

Fig. 7 plots the evolutions of [ \mathrm{s}_{22} ] and shear stress [ \mathrm{s}_{12} ] at the peak, off-peak, and off-valley locations under trapezoidal load waveforms. As the cycle number increases, [ \mathrm{s}_{12} ] at the off-valley location initially increases and then decreases because of the viscoplastic behavior of TBCs. Cracks are likely initiated at off-valley locations in the first few cycles due to the high [ \mathrm{s}_{12} ] level and propagate along the TC/TGO interface. The magnitudes of [ \mathrm{s}_{12} ] and [ \mathrm{s}_{22} ] at the off-peak location simultaneously increase, and the increases are mainly induced by the expansion of TGO. The results indicate that the hybrid mode 1 and mode 2 cracks are produced at an off-peak location when the thermal cycles are increased to a certain value.

As shown in Figs. 8 and 9, the damage is initiated at the off-valley and off-peak locations of the interfacial curve and is extended along the TC/TGO interface. Finally, the failure of TC/TGO interface will lead to TBCs spallation. In previous research [33], different failure modes are obtained under different TCF load waveforms. This finding can be attributed to a competition between microcrack growth in the TC layer and at the TC/TGO interface. Under a triangular waveform, microcracks are initiated on the top of interface curve and extend along the [ x ]-direction in the TC layer. The result indicates that failures are likely to occur in the TC layers at short holding times [ (<1\ \mathrm{s}) ], as shown in Fig. 10(a). However, in a trapezoidal load, cracks tend to extend along the interface curve, which usually has a high [ \mathrm{s}_{22} ] value (Fig. 10(b)). During actual service of TBCs, the load case is similar to the trapezoidal waveform. Thus, the interfacial strength of TC/TGO interface must be improved to increase the service life of TBCs.

Moreover, the experiments of Scrivani et al. [34] have revealed that the high content of porosity in the top coat is able to relax the compressive stress accumulated during trapezoidal thermal cycling, which can increase the thermal fatigue resistance of TBCs. However, under the triangular load waveform, the porosity of top coat has no effect on the thermal fatigue lifetime. The main factor of the thermal fatigue resistance is the interlamellar bonding strength in the TC layer [35]. Therefore, for TBCs working under a trapezoidal loading waveform, the TC layers with higher porosity have a better thermal fatigue resistance. But under triangular waveform, the better interlamellar bonding strength in the TC layer can delay the crack initiation, which can prolong the thermal fatigue lifetime of TBCs. In addition, the higher porosity will lead to degradation in interlamellar strength of TC layer, which can favor a crack growth in the TC layer. By this way, the thermal fatigue resistance decreases obviously when the porosity of the TC layer is [ >17.6\% ] under triangular waveform [36].

### 3.2. Influence of prior oxidation time under trapezoidal loading waveform

The initial thickness of the TGO is controlled by prior oxidation time of the sprayed BC layer. The TCF lifetime of TBCs is significantly influenced by initial TGO thickness [37]. Moreover, the results also reveal there exist a critical initial TGO thickness, whereby the TCF life is reduced considerably with further increase in TGO initial thickness. And the failure locations of TBCs move from the TC layer to the TC/TGO interface as the pretreatment time increases.

The simulation in this section used the different initial TGO thickness to represent a different prior oxidation time of the BC layer. The relationship between TGO thickness and prior oxidation time has been extensively investigated [38-40]. In the current work, three different initial TGO thicknesses are selected for the simulation of different prior oxidation times, namely, 9, 20, and [ 36\ \mathrm{h} ] at [ 1000^{\circ}\mathrm{C} ].

The FEM results of TBCs with initial TGO thicknesses of 1.0, 1.3, and [ 1.6\ \mu\mathrm{m} ] are shown in Fig. 11. High stress emerges at the off-peak location of TC/TGO interface (Point A). The magnitude of [ \mathrm{s}_{12} ] in the TC with thick initial TGO layers is greater than that with thin TGO layers. Fig. 11 shows that the max [ \mathrm{s}_{12} ] emerges in the vicinity of the peak of the TC layer. Nevertheless, the stress distributions in the three pre-exposure conditions are not very different, implying that the [ \mathrm{s}_{12} ] distribution is unaffected by the initial TGO thickness. Fig. 12 illustrates the evolution of shear stress [ \mathrm{s}_{12} ] of off-peak location in the TC layer. The longer the prior time (i.e. the thicker the TGO), the higher is the maximum [ \mathrm{s}_{12} ] in the TC layer. The shear stress is likely affected by the creep relaxation behavior of the TBCs. As shown in Fig. 12, for TBCs with [ 1\ \mu\mathrm{m} ] initial TGO, [ \mathrm{s}_{12} ] keeps constant after holding for [ 1500\ \mathrm{s} ], whereas those with an initial TGO thickness [ >1\ \mu\mathrm{m} ] may require a longer holding time to achieve a constant stress state. The inelastic strain of the TC layer at critical positions results from the creep due to the viscoelastic behavior of TBCs. Inelastic strain accumulation can lead to the initiation and growth of TC/TGO interface cracks. The FEM simulation results reveal that the critical initial TGO thickness is [ 1.0\ \mu\mathrm{m} ], below which stress magnitude is low, and above which the stress considerably increases in the first few cycles, as plotted in Fig. 12. Thus, the TCF life of TBCs with an initial TGO thickness [ >1\ \mu\mathrm{m} ] will substantially decrease.

### 3.3. Influence of hold time under trapezoidal loading waveform

Trapezoidal loading waveform with various holding times can cause a different stress distribution that is induced by viscoelastic behavior and accumulated interface damage in each cycle because holding time at high temperature is one of the major factors that contribute to TGO growth. The effect of holding time on stress distribution and interfacial damage evolution was explored using more than four thermal loading waveforms. The initial thickness of TGO layers was set as [ 0.7\ \mu\mathrm{m} ]. We assumed that the holding times at high temperatures were 0.00028, 0.186, 0.27, and [ 2\ \mathrm{h} ]. Details on the other FE models are similar. Notably, for [ 2\ \mathrm{h} ] of holding time, the calculation was stopped after all the interfacial cohesive zone model (CZM) elements failed. Owing to the effects of viscoelastic behavior and interfacial damage in each thermal cycle, stress developments follow those in previous research works, as discussed by Li et al. [33] and Ranjbar-Far et al. [41].

Stress fields [ \mathrm{s}_{12} ] in the TC layer with different holding times are plotted in Fig. 13. Holding time considerably influences stress distributions. The increased holding time and thickness of TGO dramatically contribute to the increase of [ \mathrm{s}_{12} ]. As shown in Fig. 13, the stress [ \mathrm{s}_{12} ] concentration (SSC) region initially appears at the off-peak location on the TC/TGO interfacial curve. As dwelling time increases, the SSC region moves into the TC layer, close to the off-valley location of the TC/TGO interface. The [ \mathrm{s}_{22} ] field in the TC layer is illustrated in Fig. 15. The location of maximum [ \mathrm{s}_{22} ] transforms from the peak of TC/TGO interface to the middle of the asperity curve. In Section 3.1, the results reveal that interfacial cracks are likely to initially occur at off-valley locations. The results of [ \mathrm{s}_{22} ] and [ \mathrm{s}_{12} ] at the off-valley location are related to number of cycles, as plotted in Figs. 14 and 16. As the number of thermal cycles increases, the [ \mathrm{s}_{22} ] becomes more dependent on holding time, as presented in Fig. 14. Furthermore, due to the competition between TGO growth rate and material creep behavior, when the hold time reaches [ 2400\ \mathrm{s} ] the stress [ \mathrm{s}_{12} ] decreases after the 4th cycles. However, the material creep behavior does not seem to significantly contribute to the evolution of [ \mathrm{s}_{22} ]. This observation has been noted by Ranjbar-Far et al. [19] and Bialas et al. [41].

Thus, the possibility of crack initiation along the TC/TGO interface is increased when holding time is prolonged. In Section 3.1, the cracks are initiated at the off-valley location of the TC/TGO interface. The damage evolution of this location is highlighted in Fig. 17, where the damage parameter [ D ] is defined according to Eq. (4). As holding time increases, the damage for each cycle rapidly grows, indicating a short TCF life of TBCs under long holding time. Moreover, Li et al. [33] and Saucedo-Mora et al. [42] showed that the increase of holding time at high temperature increases oxidation damage in every thermal cycle, which further decreases TCF life.

## 4. Conclusions

In this work, numerical simulation is conducted on stress development related to the growth of the TGO layer. The roles of load waveform, prior oxidation time, and holding time at maximum temperature during the thermal fatigue cycles are assessed. The influence of TGO on stress development is clarified. We found that cracks are initiated at the peak of TGO/TC interface and extended in the TC layers with a triangular load waveform. Nevertheless, the crack is likely to initiate at the off-valley locations and propagate along the TC/TGO interface under a trapezoidal load waveform. The TGO growth stress development is influenced by material creep behaviors under trapezoidal waveform. Given that the stress value produced by a thick initial TGO is much greater than that caused by a thin one in the first few thermal cycles, TBCs with thicker initial TGO layers under trapezoidal load waveform will suffer earlier failure. With different holding time under trapezoidal waveform, the initiation of cracks in the TBCs turns from the TGO/TC interface to the inside of TC layers when the dwelling time is increased. The possibility of crack initiation is increased as holding time is prolonged because of the interfacial damage increasing in per cycle. Thus, achieving TBCs with better interlamellar bonding strength in the TC layer as a result of the thermal spraying process will result in improved TCF resistance both in triangular and trapezoidal load waveform. For TBCs, serviced under trapezoidal waveform, the critical initial TGO thickness is [ 1\ \mu\mathrm{m} ] above which the lifetime of the TBCs will considerably decrease.
